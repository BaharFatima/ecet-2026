📌 Atomic Structure

Atomic Number (Z): Number of protons in the nucleus. Determines the element. Mass Number (A): Protons + Neutrons. Electrons = Protons (in neutral atom).
Isotopes: Same Z, different A (same element, different neutrons). e.g., ¹H, ²H(deuterium), ³H(tritium).
Isobars: Same A, different Z. e.g., ⁴⁰Ca and ⁴⁰Ar (same mass number, different elements).

Bohr's Atomic Model (for H-like atoms)
Key postulates: 1. Electrons move in fixed circular orbits (stationary states) without radiating energy. 2. Only certain orbits are allowed — those where angular momentum = nh/(2π), n=1,2,3... 3. Energy is emitted or absorbed only when electron jumps between orbits. Energy of nth orbit: Eₙ = −13.6/n² eV (for Hydrogen) Radius of nth orbit: rₙ = 0.529n² Å (Bohr radius for n=1 is 0.529 Å) Velocity in nth orbit: vₙ = 2.18×10⁶/n m/s Limitation: Cannot explain multi-electron atoms, fine structure, or Zeeman effect.
Orbitals and Quantum Numbers
Principal quantum number (n): shell number, n=1,2,3,4... (K,L,M,N) Azimuthal (l): subshell; l=0(s), 1(p), 2(d), 3(f) Magnetic (mₗ): orientation; −l to +l Spin (mₛ): +½ or −½ s-orbital: spherical, 1 orbital, holds 2 electrons p-orbital: dumbbell shape, 3 orbitals (pₓ,pᵧ,p_z), holds 6 electrons d-orbital: complex shapes, 5 orbitals, holds 10 electrons f-orbital: 7 orbitals, holds 14 electrons
Electronic Configuration Rules
Aufbau Principle: Fill orbitals from lowest to highest energy. Energy order: 1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p... Memory: use (n+l) rule — lower (n+l) fills first; equal (n+l) → lower n first. Pauli's Exclusion Principle: No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers. Max 2 electrons per orbital (opposite spins). Hund's Rule: In degenerate orbitals (same energy, e.g., 2p), electrons fill one-by-one with parallel spins before pairing. Example: C (Z=6): 1s²2s²2p² → the 2 electrons in 2p go into 2 different p orbitals Examples: Na (Z=11): 1s²2s²2p⁶3s¹ [or [Ne]3s¹] Fe (Z=26): [Ar]3d⁶4s² Cu (Z=29): [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ ← exception! (full d is more stable)

📌 Chemical Bonding

Types of Chemical Bonds
Ionic Bond: Transfer of electrons from metal to non-metal. Metal gives electrons (oxidized) → cation; Non-metal takes (reduced) → anion. Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. Properties: high MP/BP, crystalline solid, conducts electricity when dissolved/molten. Example: NaCl (Na→Na⁺ + e⁻; Cl + e⁻→Cl⁻) Covalent Bond: Sharing of electron pairs between non-metals. Single bond: 1 shared pair (e.g., H₂, CH₄) Double bond: 2 shared pairs (e.g., O₂, CO₂) Triple bond: 3 shared pairs (e.g., N₂, C₂H₂) Properties: low BP, can be gas/liquid/solid, poor electrical conductor. Co-ordinate (Dative) Covalent Bond: Both electrons of the shared pair come from ONE atom (donor/Lewis base). Example: NH₄⁺ (NH₃ donates lone pair to H⁺), H₃O⁺, BF₃·NH₃ complex. Metallic Bond: Positive metal ions in a "sea" of delocalized electrons. Explains conductivity, malleability, ductility, metallic lustre. Hydrogen Bond: Electrostatic attraction between δ+ H (bonded to F,O,N) and lone pair on F,O,N Intermolecular H-bond: between different molecules (e.g., water-water: causes high BP) Intramolecular H-bond: within same molecule (e.g., o-nitrophenol: lowers BP) H-bond is MUCH weaker than covalent but critically important in biology (DNA, proteins)

📌 Oxidation-Reduction (Redox)

Oxidation: loss of electrons / increase in oxidation number Reduction: gain of electrons / decrease in oxidation number (OIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain) Oxidation Number Rules: 1. Free element = 0 (e.g., O₂=0, Fe=0) 2. Monoatomic ion = charge (e.g., Fe³⁺ = +3) 3. H = +1 (except in metal hydrides where H = −1, e.g., NaH) 4. O = −2 (except in peroxides O₂²⁻: O=−1; OF₂: O=+2) 5. F always = −1 (most electronegative) 6. Sum of oxidation numbers = overall charge Examples: Cr in K₂Cr₂O₇: 2(+1)+2(Cr)+7(−2)=0 → 2Cr=12 → Cr=+6 Mn in KMnO₄: (+1)+Mn+4(−2)=0 → Mn=+7 S in H₂SO₄: 2(+1)+S+4(−2)=0 → S=+6

📌 Concentration Units — Molarity & Normality

Atomic Weight: average mass of atoms of an element relative to 1/12 mass of ¹²C. Molecular Weight: sum of atomic weights of all atoms in a molecule. Equivalent Weight: molecular weight divided by valency (n-factor).

Mole: 1 mole = 6.022×10²³ particles (Avogadro's number) Number of moles = mass(g) / molar mass(g/mol) = volume(L) / 22.4 L (for gas at STP) Molarity (M): moles of solute per litre of solution M = n(solute) / V(solution in L) M = mass(g) / [MW × V(L)] Normality (N): gram equivalents of solute per litre of solution N = gram equivalents / V(L) N = mass(g) / [EW × V(L)] where EW = equivalent weight = MW / n-factor Relation: N = M × n-factor Acid: n-factor = number of H⁺ ions it can donate Base: n-factor = number of OH⁻ ions it can provide For dilution: M₁V₁ = M₂V₂ (moles conserved)
Numerical Example

Find molarity of H₂SO₄ solution: 49g dissolved in 500 mL. MW of H₂SO₄ = 2+32+64 = 98 g/mol Moles = 49/98 = 0.5 mol Volume = 500 mL = 0.5 L Molarity = 0.5/0.5 = 1 M Normality: n-factor of H₂SO₄ = 2 (dibasic acid) N = M × 2 = 2 N

📌 Colloids — Types and Properties

A colloid is a mixture where particles (1–100 nm) are dispersed in a medium without settling. It's intermediate between a true solution (particles <1 nm) and a suspension (particles >100 nm, settle).

Classification by dispersed phase and medium: Aerosol: solid/liquid in gas (smoke, fog, clouds) Foam: gas in liquid/solid (shaving cream, sponge) Emulsion: liquid in liquid (milk, cream, mayonnaise) Sol: solid in liquid (gold sol, starch in water) Gel: liquid in solid (gelatin, cheese, butter) Lyophilic: solvent-loving colloids (stable, e.g., starch, gelatin) Lyophobic: solvent-hating colloids (unstable, need stabilizer, e.g., gold sol) Protective Colloids: Lyophilic colloids added to stabilize lyophobic ones. Gold Number: minimum mg of protective colloid needed to prevent coagulation of 10 mL gold sol when 1 mL 10% NaCl is added. SMALLER gold number = MORE protective power. Properties of Colloids: Tyndall Effect: scattering of light beam (beam visible from the side) Brownian Motion: random zigzag motion of colloidal particles Coagulation: destabilization by adding electrolyte (Hardy-Schulze rule: higher valence ion = greater coagulating power) Electrophoresis: movement of colloidal particles under electric field

📌 Theories of Acids and Bases

TheoryAcidBaseLimitation
ArrheniusGives H⁺ in waterGives OH⁻ in waterOnly for aqueous solutions
Bronsted-LowryProton (H⁺) donorProton acceptorOnly proton transfer; excludes non-proton acids
LewisElectron pair acceptorElectron pair donorMost general; applicable everywhere

Conjugate acid-base pairs: When an acid donates H⁺, the species formed is its conjugate base. e.g., HCl (acid) → Cl⁻ (conjugate base); NH₃ (base) → NH₄⁺ (conjugate acid).

📌 pH Scale & Ionic Product of Water

Ionic product of water: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C In pure water: [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷ mol/L pH = −log[H⁺] (power of Hydrogen) pOH = −log[OH⁻] pH + pOH = 14 (at 25°C) pH < 7: acidic solution ([H⁺] > 10⁻⁷) pH = 7: neutral pH > 7: basic/alkaline Strong acid (fully dissociates): [H⁺] = concentration of acid e.g., 0.01 M HCl → [H⁺] = 0.01 = 10⁻² → pH = 2 Strong base: [OH⁻] = concentration of base → pOH = −log[OH⁻] → pH = 14−pOH e.g., 0.001 M NaOH → [OH⁻] = 10⁻³ → pOH=3 → pH=11
pH Numerical Examples

① Find pH of 0.001 M H₂SO₄ (strong, diprotic): [H⁺] = 2×0.001 = 0.002 = 2×10⁻³ mol/L pH = −log(2×10⁻³) = −log2 − log10⁻³ = −0.301+3 = 2.699 ≈ 2.7 ② Find pH of 0.1 M NaOH: [OH⁻] = 0.1 = 10⁻¹; pOH = 1; pH = 14−1 = 13

📌 Buffer Solutions

A buffer solution resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. Composed of a weak acid + its salt with a strong base (acidic buffer) OR weak base + its salt with a strong acid (basic buffer).

Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation: pH = pKa + log([Salt]/[Acid]) for acidic buffer pOH = pKb + log([Salt]/[Base]) for basic buffer Example: CH₃COOH/CH₃COONa buffer (acetic acid + sodium acetate) pKa of CH₃COOH = 4.74 pH = 4.74 + log([CH₃COONa]/[CH₃COOH]) Equal concentrations → pH = pKa = 4.74 Buffer Capacity: maximum amount of acid or base that can be added without significant pH change. Maximum capacity when [Salt]=[Acid] (pH=pKa). Applications: Blood (pH maintained at 7.4 by H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ buffer), enzyme reactions, pharmaceutical formulations, food preservation.

📌 Ecosystem & Biogeochemical Cycles

Ecosystem = biotic (living) + abiotic (non-living) components interacting as a system. Producers (plants) → Consumers (herbivores→carnivores) → Decomposers (bacteria, fungi).

Food Chain: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle Energy flows one way; only ~10% transfers to next level (10% rule) Food Web: Multiple interconnected food chains (more realistic) Carbon Cycle: CO₂ → photosynthesis → glucose → respiration → CO₂ Also: decomposition, combustion, ocean absorption Nitrogen Cycle: N₂ → nitrogen fixation (Rhizobium bacteria) → NH₃ → NO₃⁻ → plant uptake → proteins → decomposition → N₂ (denitrification) Biodiversity: variety of species in an ecosystem. Threats: habitat loss, climate change, pollution, invasive species, overexploitation. Deforestation: loss of trees → soil erosion, climate change, biodiversity loss. Green Chemistry: design chemical processes that reduce/eliminate use or generation of hazardous substances. 12 principles (atom economy, catalysis etc.) E-Waste Management: segregation, collection, formal recycling (not burning/dumping). Contains valuable metals (Au, Ag, Cu) but also toxic (Pb, Hg, Cd).

📌 Hardness of Water

Water is hard when it does not readily form lather with soap. Caused by dissolved calcium (Ca²⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺) salts. These ions react with soap (sodium stearate) to form insoluble scum instead of lather.

Temporary Hardness: caused by Ca(HCO₃)₂ and Mg(HCO₃)₂ Removed by: Boiling → Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O + CO₂ Clarke's method (add lime Ca(OH)₂) Permanent Hardness: caused by CaSO₄, CaCl₂, MgSO₄, MgCl₂ Cannot be removed by boiling. Needs chemical/exchange treatment. Removed by: Washing soda (Na₂CO₃) treatment Ion exchange (zeolite/permutit) process Synthetic resin ion exchange Degree of Hardness: expressed as ppm of CaCO₃ equivalent ppm = mg of CaCO₃ equivalent per litre of water Soft water: 0–75 ppm; Hard: >150 ppm

📌 Softening Methods

Permutit (Zeolite) Process: Zeolite (Na₂Al₂Si₂O₈·nH₂O = Na₂Ze) exchanges Na⁺ for Ca²⁺,Mg²⁺ Ca²⁺ + Na₂Ze → CaZe + 2Na⁺ (water softened) Regeneration: CaZe + 2NaCl → Na₂Ze + CaCl₂ (using brine) Limitation: Cannot remove acidic/alkaline impurities. Ion Exchange (Deionization): Two resin columns: cation exchanger (R-H) and anion exchanger (R-OH) Cation: Ca²⁺ + 2R-H → R₂Ca + 2H⁺ Anion: SO₄²⁻ + 2R-OH → R₂SO₄ + 2OH⁻ H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O (nearly pure water obtained) Reverse Osmosis (RO): Applied pressure > osmotic pressure forces water through semi-permeable membrane. Membrane blocks dissolved salts, ions, bacteria. Produces very pure water. Used in drinking water purification. Osmosis: water moves naturally from dilute to concentrated side. Reverse osmosis: PRESSURE forces water from concentrated to dilute. Desalination by Electrodialysis: Salt water placed between ion-selective membranes. Under electric field, cations move toward cathode, anions toward anode. Fresh water region in the middle. Used for seawater desalination. Defluoridation (Nalgonda Technique): Removes excess fluoride (>1.5 ppm) from drinking water. Add alum (Al₂(SO₄)₃) + lime (Ca(OH)₂) → flocculation → settle/filter. Used in rural Telangana and Andhra Pradesh.

📌 Electrolysis & Faraday's Laws

Electrolysis is the decomposition of a compound (electrolyte) by passing direct electric current through its solution or melt. The cathode (−) attracts cations (reduced); the anode (+) attracts anions (oxidized).

Electrolysis of Fused NaCl: At cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (sodium metal deposited) At anode: Cl⁻ → (1/2)Cl₂ + e⁻ (chlorine gas evolved) Used in Downs process to manufacture sodium metal. Electrolysis of Aqueous NaCl (brine): At cathode: 2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂↑ + 2OH⁻ (H₂ gas) At anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂↑ + 2e⁻ (Cl₂ gas) Solution becomes NaOH (Chlor-alkali process) Arrhenius Theory of Electrolytic Dissociation: Electrolytes dissociate into ions when dissolved in water. Strong electrolytes: complete dissociation (HCl, NaOH, NaCl) Weak electrolytes: partial dissociation (CH₃COOH, NH₃)
Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis
First Law: Mass deposited (m) ∝ quantity of electricity (Q) m = ZQ = ZIt Z = electrochemical equivalent (g/C) Second Law: When same charge passed through different electrolytes, masses deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights. m₁/m₂ = E₁/E₂ Useful Formula: m = (E/96500) × I × t where E = equivalent weight, I = current (A), t = time (s) 96500 C/mol = 1 Faraday = charge of 1 mole of electrons
Faraday's Law Example

How much silver (E=108) is deposited by 2A in 1 hour? m = (E/96500) × I × t m = (108/96500) × 2 × 3600 m = 108 × 7200/96500 = 777600/96500 ≈ 8.06 g

📌 Key Definitions

Mineral: naturally occurring compound of a metal in the earth's crust Ore: mineral from which metal can be extracted economically Gangue (Matrix): impurities mixed with ore (sand, clay, etc.) Flux: substance added to react with gangue to form easily removable slag Slag: fusible product of flux + gangue acidic flux (SiO₂) + basic gangue (CaO) → CaSiO₃ (slag) basic flux (CaO) + acidic gangue (SiO₂) → CaSiO₃ (slag)

📌 Concentration of Ore & Extraction

Concentration Methods: Gravity Separation: dense ore particles separated from lighter gangue by water flow Froth Flotation: sulfide ores; ore particles cling to froth (with pine oil), gangue sinks Magnetic Separation: magnetic ores (magnetite, Fe₃O₄) separated by magnetic field Extraction Methods: Calcination: heating carbonate/hydroxide ores WITHOUT air → oxide + CO₂ e.g., CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (limestone → lime) Roasting: heating sulfide ores IN air → oxide + SO₂ e.g., 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂ Smelting: reduction of oxide ore with coke (carbon) at high temperature e.g., ZnO + C → Zn + CO Blast Furnace (Iron extraction): Zone 1 (bottom, 1800°C): C+O₂→CO₂; CO₂+C→2CO (reducing agent) Zone 2 (middle, 700°C): Fe₂O₃+3CO→2Fe+3CO₂ (iron reduced) Zone 3 (top): CaCO₃→CaO+CO₂; CaO+SiO₂→CaSiO₃ (slag removal) Pig iron (molten Fe) collected at bottom; slag floats.

📌 Corrosion Theory & Prevention

Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of a metal due to chemical or electrochemical reactions with the environment. Rusting of iron is the most common example.

Rusting of Iron (Electrochemical/Wet Corrosion): Anodic areas: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation, iron dissolves) Cathodic areas: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (reduction, oxygen consumed) Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂ → further oxidizes → Fe₂O₃·nH₂O (rust) Factors affecting rate of corrosion: 1. Nature of metal (reactive metals corrode faster) 2. Position in electrochemical series (greater potential diff = faster corrosion) 3. Presence of electrolyte (salt water corrodes faster than pure) 4. Temperature (higher = faster) 5. pH (acidic conditions accelerate) 6. Stress in metal Types of Corrosion Cells: Composition cell: two different metals in contact + electrolyte Stress cell: stressed vs unstressed parts of same metal Concentration cell: same metal, different O₂ concentrations Prevention Methods: Cathodic protection: connect iron to more active metal (Mg, Zn) as sacrificial anode Galvanizing: coating with zinc (zinc corrodes preferentially) Tinning: coating with tin (only mechanical barrier) Painting: physical barrier (oxide primer, zinc chromate) Alloying: making stainless steel, etc. Paint Constituents: Pigment (color/opacity), Vehicle/Binder (film-forming oil), Drier (accelerates drying), Thinner (adjusts viscosity), Extender (CaCO₃, BaSO₄)

📌 Polymerization Types

Addition Polymerization: Monomers with C=C double bond add to each other without losing any atoms. No by-product formed. Monomer = repeat unit. e.g., nCH₂=CH₂ → (-CH₂-CH₂-)ₙ (Polyethylene/Polythene) Condensation Polymerization: Monomers with two functional groups react with elimination of small molecules (H₂O, HCl, etc.) from each step. e.g., Nylon 6,6: hexamethylenediamine + adipic acid → Nylon + nH₂O

📌 Plastics — Types and Examples

Thermoplastics: soften on heating, harden on cooling (reversible) Can be recycled. Long linear chains. Examples: Polythene, PVC, Polystyrene, Teflon, Nylon, Polyester Thermosetting: permanently harden on heating (irreversible, cross-linked 3D network) Cannot be remoulded once set. Examples: Bakelite, Urea-formaldehyde, Melamine Important Plastics: Polythene (polyethylene): CH₂=CH₂ → LDPE (low density, flexible, bags) HDPE (high density, rigid, pipes, bottles) PVC (polyvinyl chloride): CH₂=CHCl → pipes, flooring, insulation, records Teflon (PTFE): CF₂=CF₂ → non-stick, chemically inert, low friction; cooking utensils Polystyrene: CH₂=CH-C₆H₅ → transparent, rigid; disposable cups, insulation foam Urea-formaldehyde: urea + formaldehyde → electrical fittings, adhesives, laminates Bakelite: phenol + formaldehyde → first synthetic plastic; electrical insulator, handles

📌 Rubber & Synthetic Elastomers

Natural Rubber: polymer of isoprene (2-methylbutadiene); C₅H₈ units Soft, sticky, not durable; poor in heat/cold/chemicals. Vulcanization: heating natural rubber with sulfur (2-8%) Sulfur cross-links polymer chains → stronger, elastic, non-sticky, durable. Hard rubber (ebonite): 30-50% sulfur. Synthetic Rubbers: Buna-S (SBR): butadiene + styrene; general-purpose tyres Buna-N (Nitrile rubber): butadiene + acrylonitrile; oil-resistant gaskets, gloves Neoprene (Polychloroprene): chloroprene polymerization; weather/oil resistant; wetsuits Butyl rubber: isobutylene + isoprene; low gas permeability; inner tubes

📌 Fibres & Biodegradable Polymers

Nylon 6,6 (Polyamide): Monomers: Hexamethylenediamine (H₂N-(CH₂)₆-NH₂) + Adipic acid (HOOC-(CH₂)₄-COOH) Condensation: amide bond (CO-NH) formed with loss of H₂O Properties: strong, elastic, abrasion resistant, silk-like Uses: hosiery, ropes, parachutes, toothbrush bristles Polyester (PET — Polyethylene Terephthalate): Monomers: Ethylene glycol + Terephthalic acid → ester bond with loss of H₂O Uses: Dacron (fiber for clothes), Terylene, plastic bottles (PET bottles) Biodegradable Polymers: broken down by microorganisms PHBV (polyhydroxy butyrate-co-valerate): medical implants, packaging Nylon 2-nylon 6: alternating glycine and aminocaproic acid units

📌 Fuels — Classification & Calorific Values

Classification: Solid: coal, wood, coke, charcoal Liquid: petrol, diesel, kerosene, alcohol Gaseous: LPG, CNG, biogas, hydrogen, coal gas, water gas, producer gas Calorific Value: heat energy released per unit mass/volume of fuel HCV (Higher Calorific Value): includes latent heat of water vapor formed LCV (Lower Calorific Value): excludes latent heat (water remains as vapor) LCV = HCV − 9H × 2445 (H = mass fraction of hydrogen, 2445 kJ/kg = latent heat) Characteristics of Good Fuel: High calorific value, low ignition temperature, low cost, easy to store/transport, no harmful combustion products, burns steadily.

📌 Lubricants

Function: Reduce friction, wear, heat between moving surfaces. Also: carry away heat, prevent corrosion, seal gaps. Classification: Solid: graphite, MoS₂ (used in high temp, extreme pressure situations) Liquid/Oil: mineral oils (petroleum-derived), vegetable oils, synthetic oils Semi-solid/Grease: mineral oil + soap (sodium/lithium stearate thickener) Viscosity: most important property of liquid lubricant. Too thin (low viscosity) → oil squeezes out → metal-to-metal contact Too thick (high viscosity) → energy wasted churning oil → overheating Flash Point: lowest temperature at which oil gives ignitable vapor. High flash point = safer (less fire risk). Pour Point: lowest temperature at which oil flows freely. Important for cold-climate applications.

📌 Galvanic Cell & EMF

Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell: spontaneous redox reaction → electrical energy Anode (−): oxidation occurs (loses electrons) [AN OX] Cathode (+): reduction occurs (gains electrons) [RED CAT] Salt bridge: maintains electrical neutrality by ion flow between half-cells Standard Electrode Potential (E°): measured vs Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE, E°=0V) Positive E°: species is reduced relative to H₂ (good oxidizing agent) Negative E°: species oxidized relative to H₂ (good reducing agent) EMF of cell: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = E°reduction − E°oxidation Daniel Cell: Zn|ZnSO₄||CuSO₄|Cu Anode: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (E°= −0.76V) Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (E°= +0.34V) E°cell = 0.34 − (−0.76) = 1.10V

📌 Air, Water & Soil Pollution

Air Pollution Sources & Effects: CO (carbon monoxide): incomplete combustion; binds to haemoglobin (carboxyhemoglobin) SO₂: burning coal; causes acid rain (SO₂+H₂O→H₂SO₃), respiratory problems NOₓ: vehicle exhaust; photochemical smog, acid rain Particulates (PM2.5, PM10): respiratory diseases, visibility reduction CFC: ozone layer depletion → increased UV → skin cancer CO₂: greenhouse gas → global warming Control: Catalytic converters, scrubbers, electrostatic precipitators, CNG vehicles Water Pollution: Industrial effluents: heavy metals (Hg, Pb, Cd), acids, BOD increase Agricultural: fertilizers (nitrate runoff → eutrophication), pesticides Sewage: pathogens, BOD increase, DO decrease Thermal: warm water from power plants → less dissolved O₂ Eutrophication: excess nutrients → algal bloom → oxygen depletion → fish death BOD (Biological Oxygen Demand): O₂ needed to decompose organic waste; high BOD = more pollution Soil Pollution: Causes: pesticides, industrial waste, plastic waste, mining, acid rain Effects: reduced soil fertility, bioaccumulation in food chain Control: organic farming, bioremediation, composting Carbon Trading: market-based approach where companies buy/sell carbon emission permits Cap-and-trade system reduces total emissions economically.
