Introduction to Environmental Science
Environment • Scope • Importance • Key Terms

🌍What is Environment?

Environment refers to everything surrounding a living organism — the sum total of all living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) conditions and influences that affect an organism's life and development.

Key Definition: "The word 'Environment' is derived from the French word Environner meaning 'to surround'."
  • Natural Environment: Land, water, air, plants, animals
  • Human-made Environment: Cities, roads, industries
  • Social Environment: Cultural, economic, political conditions

📐Scope of Environmental Studies

Environmental studies is multidisciplinary — it draws from biology, chemistry, physics, geology, economics, and social sciences.

  • Study of natural resources and their conservation
  • Understanding pollution and its control
  • Study of biodiversity and its protection
  • Social and economic dimensions of environment
  • Environmental laws and policies

⭐Importance of Environmental Studies

  • Helps understand ecological relationships
  • Creates awareness about environmental degradation
  • Promotes sustainable development
  • Trains people for environmental management
  • Addresses global issues: climate change, ozone depletion, acid rain
📌 Exam Tip: Questions often ask about the multidisciplinary nature of environmental studies and its importance in sustainable development.

Concept of Ecosystem
Producers • Consumers • Decomposers

🌿What is an Ecosystem?

An ecosystem is a functional unit of nature where living organisms (biotic) interact with each other and with their non-living (abiotic) environment, with energy flowing and nutrients cycling through the system.

Coined by: A.G. Tansley in 1935.
Simplest example: A pond. Largest: Biosphere.
  • Types: Terrestrial (forest, grassland, desert) and Aquatic (pond, lake, ocean)
  • Energy flows unidirectionally (Sun → Plants → Animals)
  • Nutrients cycle repeatedly (biogeochemical cycles)

🌱Producers (Autotrophs)

Organisms that produce their own food through photosynthesis or chemosynthesis. They form the base of all food chains.

  • Green plants, algae, phytoplankton
  • Use sunlight + CO₂ + H₂O → Glucose + O₂
  • Also called Autotrophs (self-feeders)
Formula: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

🐾Consumers (Heterotrophs)

Organisms that cannot make their own food and depend on producers or other consumers.

TypeAlso CalledExample
Primary ConsumersHerbivoresGrasshopper, Rabbit, Deer, Cow
Secondary ConsumersCarnivores / OmnivoresFrog, Fox, Small fish
Tertiary ConsumersTop CarnivoresSnake, Eagle, Lion, Tiger
OmnivoresBoth plant & animalHuman, Bear, Crow

🍄Decomposers (Saprotrophs)

Organisms that break down dead organic matter (detritus) into simple inorganic substances, recycling nutrients back into the ecosystem.

  • Examples: Bacteria, Fungi (mushrooms, molds)
  • Also called Reducers or Saprotrophic organisms
  • They close the nutrient cycle — essential for soil fertility
  • Convert complex organic → simple inorganic (CO₂, H₂O, minerals)
📌 Exam Tip: Decomposers are bacteria and fungi. They are NOT consumers. This is a common MCQ confusion!

⚡Energy Flow in Ecosystem

Only about 10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next — this is called the 10% Law (given by Lindeman, 1942).

Sun (Solar Energy)
→
Producers (10,000 kcal)
→
Herbivores (1,000 kcal)
→
Carnivores (100 kcal)
→
Top Carnivores (10 kcal)
10% Law: 90% of energy is lost at each trophic level as heat. Only 10% moves to the next level.
Food Chain & Food Web
Linear and complex feeding relationships

🔗Food Chain

A food chain is a linear sequence showing how energy and nutrients are transferred from one organism to another through feeding.

Example (Grassland): Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Eagle
Example (Pond): Algae → Water flea → Small fish → Large fish → Heron
  • Always begins with a Producer
  • Energy flows in one direction
  • Each step is a Trophic Level
  • Usually has 3–5 trophic levels (limited by 10% energy law)

🍃Types of Food Chains

TypeStarts WithExample
Grazing Food ChainLiving green plantsGrass → Deer → Lion
Detritus Food ChainDead organic matterDead leaves → Earthworm → Bird
📌 Exam Tip: Grazing food chain starts with producers. Detritus food chain starts with dead organic matter. Know this difference!

🕸️Food Web

A food web is a network of interconnected food chains in an ecosystem. It shows the complex feeding relationships more realistically.

  • More realistic representation of nature
  • An organism can be at multiple trophic levels
  • Greater the complexity, greater the stability of the ecosystem
  • If one organism is removed, the ecosystem can still survive (unlike food chain)
Key Difference: Food chain = single path. Food web = multiple interconnected paths. Food web provides STABILITY to the ecosystem.
📌 Exam Tip: Food web provides more stability than food chain — frequently asked MCQ!

🏔️Ecological Pyramids

PyramidDescriptionAlways Upright?
Pyramid of NumbersNumber of organisms at each trophic levelNot always (inverted in parasitic chain)
Pyramid of BiomassTotal dry weight at each trophic levelUsually upright (inverted in aquatic)
Pyramid of EnergyAmount of energy at each trophic levelAlways upright (never inverted)
📌 Exam Tip: Pyramid of Energy is ALWAYS upright — this is a guaranteed MCQ question!

🔄Carbon Cycle

Carbon cycles through the biotic and abiotic components of the ecosystem. The primary reservoir is the atmosphere (CO₂) and oceans.

📌 Exam Tip: Which process removes CO₂ from atmosphere? → Photosynthesis. Which adds CO₂? → Respiration, Combustion, Decomposition.

🔵Nitrogen Cycle

Nitrogen makes up 78% of the atmosphere but most organisms cannot use atmospheric N₂ directly. It must be converted into usable forms.

Key Processes:

ProcessDone ByConversion
Nitrogen FixationRhizobium (legume roots), Azotobacter, LightningN₂ → NH₃ / NH₄⁺
NitrificationNitrosomonas, Nitrobacter (bacteria)NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻
AssimilationPlants and animalsNO₃⁻ → Organic N (proteins)
AmmonificationDecomposer bacteria/fungiOrganic N → NH₄⁺
DenitrificationPseudomonas, ThiobacillusNO₃⁻ → N₂ (back to atmosphere)
Important: Rhizobium bacteria live in ROOT NODULES of leguminous plants (peas, beans, clover) and fix atmospheric nitrogen.
📌 Exam Tip: Rhizobium → nitrogen fixation in legume roots. Nitrosomonas converts NH₄⁺ to NO₂⁻. Nitrobacter converts NO₂⁻ to NO₃⁻. These are high-frequency MCQs!

Biodiversity
Definition, Types & Threats

🦋What is Biodiversity?

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life on Earth — the diversity of genes, species, and ecosystems. The term was popularized by E.O. Wilson in 1986.

Definition: "The variability among living organisms from all sources including terrestrial, marine, and other aquatic ecosystems and the ecological complexes of which they are part." — UN Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), 1992
  • India is one of the world's 17 Megadiverse countries
  • Estimated ~8.7 million species on Earth; only ~1.5 million described

🧬Types of Biodiversity

TypeDefinitionExample
Genetic DiversityVariation within a species at gene levelDifferent varieties of rice (1000+ varieties in India)
Species DiversityVariety of different species in an areaNumber of bird, insect, plant species in a forest
Ecosystem DiversityVariety of habitats/ecosystemsForests, wetlands, coral reefs, deserts
📌 Exam Tip: Three levels of biodiversity: Genetic, Species, Ecosystem. Know examples for each!

⚠️Threats to Biodiversity

The primary causes of biodiversity loss are remembered by the acronym HIPPO:

  • H – Habitat Destruction/Loss: Deforestation, urbanization, agriculture expansion (BIGGEST THREAT)
  • I – Invasive species: Non-native species outcompete native species (e.g., Lantana in Indian forests)
  • P – Pollution: Air, water, soil pollution kills organisms
  • P – Population growth: Increased human demand on resources
  • O – Overhunting/Overexploitation: Hunting, poaching, overfishing
Also important: Climate Change, Acid rain, Ozone depletion

Conservation Strategies:

MethodDescriptionExample
In-situ ConservationProtection in natural habitatWildlife sanctuaries, National parks, Biosphere reserves
Ex-situ ConservationProtection outside natural habitatZoos, Botanical gardens, Gene banks, Seed banks
📌 Exam Tip: In-situ = in natural place (National Park). Ex-situ = outside (Zoo, Seed bank). This is a very common MCQ!
Hotspot: An area with high biodiversity + high threat. India has 4 biodiversity hotspots: Western Ghats, Eastern Himalayas, Indo-Burma, Sundaland.
Forest Resources & Deforestation
Importance of forests and effects of deforestation

🌳Forest Resources – Importance

  • Regulates climate – absorbs CO₂, releases O₂
  • Watershed protection – prevents floods and soil erosion
  • Biodiversity habitat – home to 70%+ of all terrestrial species
  • Economic resources – timber, firewood, medicinal plants, resins
  • Tribal livelihoods – millions depend on forests
  • Soil conservation – roots hold soil, prevent erosion
India's Forest Cover: ~21.71% of total geographical area (target: 33% as per National Forest Policy 1988)

🪓Deforestation

Deforestation is the clearing, destruction, or removal of forests for other land uses.

Causes:

  • Agricultural expansion – slash and burn agriculture (Jhum cultivation)
  • Commercial logging – timber, paper industry
  • Urbanization – infrastructure development
  • Mining – removal of overburden (trees)
  • Fuel wood collection – especially in rural areas
  • Over-grazing – cattle in forests

Effects of Deforestation:

  • Climate change – increased CO₂, reduced rainfall
  • Soil erosion – floods and landslides
  • Loss of biodiversity – habitat destruction
  • Disrupted water cycle – reduced transpiration
  • Desertification – fertile land turns barren
  • Displacement of tribal communities
📌 Exam Tip: Jhum cultivation = slash and burn = shifting agriculture = major cause of deforestation in Northeast India.

🌿Conservation of Forests

  • Afforestation – planting trees in new areas
  • Reforestation – replanting trees in deforested areas
  • Social forestry – community involvement
  • Agroforestry – trees + crops together
  • Joint Forest Management (JFM) – government + community
Chipko Movement (1973): Women of Uttarakhand hugged trees to prevent logging — a landmark forest conservation movement in India. Led by Sunderlal Bahuguna.
Green Chemistry
Principles, Applications & Benefits

🧪What is Green Chemistry?

Green chemistry is the design of chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances. It was developed by Paul Anastas and John Warner in 1998.

Goal: Prevent pollution at source rather than treating it after it is generated. Also called "Benign by Design".
  • Also known as Sustainable Chemistry
  • Focuses on atom economy – use all atoms of reactants in product

📋12 Principles of Green Chemistry

Remember the key ones for MCQs:

#Principle
1Prevention – prevent waste rather than treat it
2Atom Economy – maximize incorporation of materials into final product
3Less Hazardous Synthesis – use/generate non-toxic substances
4Safer Chemicals – design chemicals to be effective but less toxic
5Safer Solvents – use benign (safe) solvents
6Energy Efficiency – conduct reactions at room temperature/pressure
7Renewable Feedstocks – use renewable raw materials
8Reduce Derivatives – avoid unnecessary steps
9Catalysis – use catalytic reagents
10Design for Degradation – products should break down harmlessly
11Real-time Analysis – monitor processes to prevent pollution
12Accident Prevention – minimize potential for explosions, fires
📌 Exam Tip: First principle = Prevention (prevent waste). Atom Economy (2nd principle) — formula = (MW of desired product / MW of all products) × 100

🌱Applications & Benefits

  • Biodegradable plastics (from corn starch)
  • Supercritical CO₂ as solvent instead of organic solvents
  • Bio-based fuels (ethanol from sugarcane)
  • Enzyme-based detergents (work in cold water)
  • Water-based paints instead of solvent-based
Benefits: Reduced pollution, less hazardous waste, lower energy consumption, safer workplaces, lower costs long-term.
E-Waste & Its Management
Definition, Types, Hazards & Management

💻What is E-Waste?

Electronic waste (e-waste) refers to discarded electrical and electronic equipment (EEE) that has reached the end of its useful life. It is the fastest growing waste stream in the world.

Also called: WEEE – Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment
  • Computers, laptops, mobile phones, TVs, refrigerators
  • Printers, scanners, cameras, circuit boards
  • Batteries, chargers, cables
India Fact: India is the 3rd largest e-waste producer in Asia. ~3.2 million tonnes generated annually. E-Waste Rules first notified in 2011, amended in 2016 and 2022.

🌐Global E-Waste Regulations

  • Basel Convention: International treaty controlling transboundary movement of hazardous waste including e-waste
  • RoHS Directive (EU): Restriction of Hazardous Substances — limits use of lead, mercury, cadmium in electronics
  • India E-Waste Rules 2016: Mandates EPR, targets for collection, authorized dismantlers
