Introduction to Water Technology
Soft water vs Hard water – Basic concepts

💧Why Water Technology?

Water is essential for all industrial and domestic processes. However, naturally available water contains dissolved salts, gases, and impurities that make it unsuitable for direct use. Water Technology deals with purification, softening, and treatment of water for various purposes.

Pure water: Colourless, tasteless, odourless liquid. Chemical formula H₂O. Boiling point 100°C, Freezing point 0°C at 1 atm.

🫧Soft Water vs Hard Water

The most fundamental distinction in water technology:

💧 SOFT WATER
  • Lathers easily with soap
  • Does NOT contain dissolved Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ salts
  • Suitable for laundry, boilers
  • Examples: Rain water, distilled water, demineralised water
  • Good for washing (forms foam with soap)
🪨 HARD WATER
  • Does NOT lather easily with soap
  • Contains dissolved Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ salts
  • Forms curdy/scum with soap
  • Examples: River water, well water, bore water
  • Causes wastage of soap
Key Test: Hard water forms a SCUM (insoluble white precipitate) with soap instead of lather.
Reaction: Ca²⁺ + 2RCOONa → (RCOO)₂Ca↓ + 2Na⁺ (scum)
📌 Exam Tip: Hard water does NOT lather with soap — it forms a curdy precipitate called SCUM. This is the most basic definition question!
Causes & Types of Hardness
Temporary & Permanent Hardness + Disadvantages

🔬Causes of Hardness

Hardness is caused by the presence of dissolved bicarbonates, chlorides, sulphates, and nitrates of Calcium (Ca²⁺) and Magnesium (Mg²⁺) in water.

SaltType of Hardness
Ca(HCO₃)₂ – Calcium bicarbonateTemporary
Mg(HCO₃)₂ – Magnesium bicarbonateTemporary
CaCl₂ – Calcium chloridePermanent
MgCl₂ – Magnesium chloridePermanent
CaSO₄ – Calcium sulphatePermanent
MgSO₄ – Magnesium sulphatePermanent
Ca(NO₃)₂ – Calcium nitratePermanent
Mg(NO₃)₂ – Magnesium nitratePermanent
📌 Exam Tip: ONLY bicarbonates cause temporary hardness. All others (chlorides, sulphates, nitrates) cause permanent hardness!

⏱️Types of Hardness

TEMPORARY HARDNESS
  • Caused by bicarbonates of Ca & Mg
  • Also called Carbonate Hardness
  • Can be removed by simple boiling
  • On boiling: Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O + CO₂↑
  • Also removed by Clark's process (lime)
PERMANENT HARDNESS
  • Caused by chlorides, sulphates, nitrates of Ca & Mg
  • Also called Non-Carbonate Hardness
  • Cannot be removed by boiling
  • Removed by chemical softening or ion exchange
  • More difficult and costly to remove
Total Hardness = Temporary Hardness + Permanent Hardness
📌 Exam Tip: Temporary hardness = carbonate hardness = removed by boiling. Permanent = non-carbonate = NOT removed by boiling. Very frequently asked!

🏭Disadvantages of Hard Water in Industries

  • Boiler scale formation: Dissolved salts deposit on inner walls of boilers as hard scale (mainly CaSO₄, CaCO₃, Mg(OH)₂) → reduces heat transfer → wastage of fuel
  • Boiler corrosion: MgCl₂ hydrolyses at high temp → HCl → corrodes boiler walls
  • Priming and Foaming: Dissolved salts lower surface tension → water droplets carry over with steam → priming. Foaming caused by oils/organic matter
  • Caustic embrittlement: NaOH concentrates in crevices → reacts with iron → iron becomes brittle → boiler explosions
  • Wastage of soap: More soap needed to produce lather (forms scum first)
  • Textile industry: Affects fabric quality, dye absorption
  • Paper industry: Affects paper quality
  • Food industry: Slows cooking of vegetables and pulses
⚠️ Boiler Scale: 1 mm of scale requires 2% extra fuel. 6 mm of scale needs 15% extra fuel. Extremely dangerous and costly!
📌 Exam Tip: Boiler scale is the MOST IMPORTANT disadvantage of hard water in industries. Remember: scale = poor heat conductor = more fuel needed!

Degree of Hardness
Units, Scales & Calculations

📐What is Degree of Hardness?

Degree of hardness is the numerical expression of the amount of hardness-causing salts in water. It is expressed in terms of equivalent CaCO₃.

Why CaCO₃? Calcium Carbonate (CaCO₃) is chosen as the reference because:
• Molecular weight = 100 (easy calculations)
• Equivalent weight = 50 (MW/valency = 100/2)
• It is insoluble and easy to understand

Softening Methods
Boiling, Clark's, Lime-Soda & Permutit Process

⚗️Overview of Softening Methods

MethodRemovesScale
BoilingTemporary hardness onlySmall (domestic)
Clark's process (Lime)Temporary hardness onlyMedium
Lime-Soda processBoth temporary & permanentLarge industrial
Permutit/Zeolite processBoth (but not acidic water)Industrial
Ion Exchange (Demineralization)All dissolved saltsIndustrial/Lab

🔥Boiling Method

Removes ONLY temporary hardness. On boiling, bicarbonates decompose:

Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O + CO₂↑
Mg(HCO₃)₂ → MgCO₃↓ + H₂O + CO₂↑ → Mg(OH)₂↓ + CO₂↑
Insoluble CaCO₃ and Mg(OH)₂ precipitate out. Simple, but only for small scale and removes only temporary hardness.

🧪Clark's Process (Lime Process)

Removes temporary hardness by adding calculated amount of lime (Ca(OH)₂):

Ca(HCO₃)₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2CaCO₃↓ + 2H₂O
Mg(HCO₃)₂ + 2Ca(OH)₂ → 2CaCO₃↓ + Mg(OH)₂↓ + 2H₂O
Limitation: Only removes temporary hardness. Excess lime addition re-introduces hardness. Must add exact amount.

🏭Lime-Soda Process

The most widely used industrial softening method. Uses lime [Ca(OH)₂] + soda ash [Na₂CO₃] to remove both types of hardness.

Reactions:

For temporary hardness: Ca(HCO₃)₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2CaCO₃↓ + 2H₂O Mg(HCO₃)₂ + 2Ca(OH)₂ → 2CaCO₃↓ + Mg(OH)₂↓ + 2H₂O
For permanent hardness (need soda ash): CaSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄ MgSO₄ + Ca(OH)₂ → Mg(OH)₂↓ + CaSO₄ (then CaSO₄ reacts with Na₂CO₃) CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃↓ + 2NaCl MgCl₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → Mg(OH)₂↓ + CaCl₂ (then CaCl₂ with Na₂CO₃)
Cold Lime-Soda Process
  • Done at room temperature
  • Uses coagulants (alum)
  • Residual hardness: 50-70 ppm
  • Slow process
Hot Lime-Soda Process
  • Done at ~80°C
  • Faster reactions
  • Residual hardness: 15-30 ppm
  • Better quality output
📌 Exam Tip: Lime removes Mg hardness (as Mg(OH)₂). Soda ash (Na₂CO₃) removes Ca hardness (as CaCO₃). Remember: Lime for Mg, Soda for Ca!

🟩Permutit (Zeolite) Process

Uses natural or synthetic Zeolite (sodium alumino silicate – Na₂Al₂Si₂O₈·xH₂O), also called Permutit, to soften water by ion exchange.

Zeolite Formula: Na₂Z (where Z = zeolite radical Al₂Si₂O₈·xH₂O)

Ion Exchange Reaction:

Na₂Z + CaSO₄ → CaZ↓ + Na₂SO₄ Na₂Z + MgCl₂ → MgZ↓ + 2NaCl

Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ are exchanged for Na⁺ ions. The hardness-causing ions are held by zeolite, and harmless Na⁺ goes into water.

Regeneration of Exhausted Zeolite:

CaZ + 2NaCl → Na₂Z + CaCl₂ MgZ + 2NaCl → Na₂Z + MgCl₂

Zeolite is regenerated by passing a 10% common salt (NaCl) solution through it.

✅ Advantages
  • Simple operation
  • Efficient – residual hardness near 0
  • Easy regeneration with NaCl
  • No sludge produced
  • Compact equipment
❌ Limitations
  • Cannot treat acidic water (pH < 7)
  • Turbid/muddy water clogs zeolite
  • Does not remove all dissolved salts (Na salts remain)
  • Does not remove silica, iron
📌 Exam Tip: Regeneration of zeolite uses NaCl (brine/common salt) solution. Exhausted zeolite = CaZ & MgZ. Regenerated zeolite = Na₂Z. Very common MCQ!
Ion Exchange Process
Deionization / Demineralization – Complete removal of all salts

⚡Ion Exchange / Demineralization Process

This process removes ALL dissolved mineral salts from water using two types of resins. The product is called deionized water or demineralized water (DM water).

Resins used:
• Cation Exchange Resin (H⁺ type): RH₂ (e.g., sulfonated polystyrene) — exchanges H⁺ for cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Na⁺, K⁺)
• Anion Exchange Resin (OH⁻ type): R(OH)₂ — exchanges OH⁻ for anions (Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, HCO₃⁻)

Step 1 – Cation Exchange (Cation Exchanger / H-Zeolite):

2RH + CaCl₂ → R₂Ca + 2HCl 2RH + MgSO₄ → R₂Mg + H₂SO₄ 2RH + Na₂CO₃ → R₂(2Na) + H₂CO₃

All cations replaced by H⁺ → water becomes acidic (contains HCl, H₂SO₄, H₂CO₃)

Step 2 – Anion Exchange (Anion Exchanger / OH-Zeolite):

2ROH + H₂SO₄ → R₂SO₄ + 2H₂O ROH + HCl → RCl + H₂O 2ROH + H₂CO₃ → R₂CO₃ + 2H₂O

All anions replaced by OH⁻ → H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O (pure water)

Hard Water
→
Cation ExchangerCa²⁺,Mg²⁺ → H⁺
→
Anion ExchangerCl⁻, SO₄²⁻ → OH⁻
→
Pure DM WaterH⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O

Regeneration:

ResinExhausted FormRegenerating AgentRegenerated Form
Cation resinR₂Ca, R₂MgDilute H₂SO₄ or HClRH₂ (restored)
Anion resinR₂SO₄, RClNaOH solutionR(OH)₂ (restored)
📌 Exam Tip: Cation resin regenerated by acid (H₂SO₄/HCl). Anion resin regenerated by base (NaOH). DM water = completely pure water (like distilled water). Very common MCQ!
Difference – Zeolite vs Ion Exchange: Zeolite only exchanges Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ for Na⁺ (water still has Na salts). Ion exchange removes ALL ions (cations AND anions) → truly pure water.

Municipal treatment for safe drinking water

🏙️Municipal Treatment of Water

Water from rivers, lakes, or reservoirs is treated in a water treatment plant before supply to homes. The steps are:

1. ScreeningRemove large debris
→
2. SedimentationSettle suspended particles
→
3. CoagulationAdd alum/FeSO₄

4. FiltrationSand/gravel filter
→
5. DisinfectionCl₂/ozone/UV

🔍Steps in Detail

1. Screening

Water passes through wire mesh screens to remove large floating objects — leaves, twigs, fish, garbage.

2. Sedimentation

Water is stored in large tanks. Gravity causes heavy suspended particles (sand, silt, clay) to settle. Settling time: several hours.

Plain sedimentation removes only large, heavy particles. Fine colloidal particles (clay, bacteria) do NOT settle by gravity alone → need coagulation.

3. Coagulation (Chemical Treatment)

Chemicals called coagulants are added to make colloidal particles aggregate into large clumps (flocs) that settle quickly.

CoagulantReaction
Alum [KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O]Al³⁺ + 3H₂O → Al(OH)₃↓ (floc) + 3H⁺ — most common coagulant
FeSO₄·7H₂O (Ferrous sulphate)Used with lime. Produces Fe(OH)₂ floc
FeCl₃ (Ferric chloride)Fe³⁺ + 3H₂O → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3H⁺
📌 Exam Tip: Alum is the MOST commonly used coagulant in water treatment. Al(OH)₃ is the actual coagulating species (gelatinous precipitate).

4. Filtration

Water passes through filter beds to remove remaining suspended matter, flocs, and bacteria:

Slow Sand Filter
  • Layers: coarse gravel → fine gravel → coarse sand → fine sand
  • Rate: 0.1-0.4 m/hr
  • Removes bacteria by biological layer (Schmutzdecke)
  • No pre-treatment needed
Rapid Sand Filter
  • Higher rate: 4-5 m/hr
  • Needs coagulation pre-treatment
  • Backwashing to clean
  • More common in large cities

5. Disinfection

Kills remaining pathogenic bacteria and viruses. Methods:

MethodAgentNote
ChlorinationCl₂ gas or bleaching powder [Ca(OCl)Cl]Most common, economical, residual effect
OzonationOzone (O₃)No residual effect, costly
UV treatmentUV light (253.7 nm)No chemicals, effective for clear water
BoilingHeat (100°C)For domestic/small scale
Chlorination: Cl₂ + H₂O → HOCl + HCl
HOCl → HCl + [O] (nascent oxygen kills bacteria)
Residual chlorine in distributed water = 0.2-0.5 mg/L (for safety)
📌 Exam Tip: Chlorination is the MOST WIDELY used disinfection method. Bleaching powder = Ca(OCl)Cl. Residual chlorine = 0.2 mg/L minimum in distributed water.

Osmosis & Reverse Osmosis
Principles, Advantages & Applications

🌊Osmosis

Osmosis is the spontaneous movement of solvent (water) from a region of lower solute concentration to a region of higher solute concentration through a semi-permeable membrane.

Pure Water(Low conc.)
→ flows naturally →
Semi-permeable Membrane
→
Salt Solution(High conc.)
Osmotic Pressure: The minimum pressure required to stop osmosis. Higher solute concentration = higher osmotic pressure.

🔄Reverse Osmosis (RO)

In Reverse Osmosis, water is forced to flow from high concentration (impure/salty) side to low concentration (pure) side by applying pressure greater than osmotic pressure — OPPOSITE to natural osmosis direction.

Impure/Salty Water(High pressure applied)
← forced through ←
Semi-permeable Membrane(0.0001 µm pores)
→
Pure Water(Permeate)
Key: Applied pressure > Osmotic pressure → water forced through membrane → salts/impurities REJECTED by membrane → pure water on the other side.

Membranes used in RO:

  • Cellulose Acetate (CA) – first generation, sensitive to hydrolysis
  • Thin Film Composite (TFC/TFM) – most widely used today, more durable
  • Pore size: ~0.0001 microns (removes bacteria, viruses, ions)

✅Advantages of Reverse Osmosis

  • Removes dissolved salts, heavy metals (As, Pb, Hg, F⁻, NO₃⁻)
  • Removes bacteria, viruses, cysts (microbiological safety)
  • Removes pesticides and organic compounds
  • Produces very high quality purified water
  • No chemicals required for operation
  • Relatively low energy compared to distillation
  • Can be scaled up (large industrial plants) or small (household)
  • Used for seawater desalination — most widely used method
  • Low maintenance, automatic operation
  • Produces consistent quality output
📌 Exam Tip: RO removes almost everything — salts, bacteria, viruses, heavy metals, pesticides. Pore size ~0.0001 µm. Pressure applied must be greater than osmotic pressure!

🧂What is Desalination?

Desalination is the process of removing dissolved salts (mainly NaCl) from seawater or brackish water to produce fresh, potable water.

Seawater salinity: ~35,000 mg/L TDS (total dissolved solids)
Potable water: TDS < 500 mg/L (WHO guideline)
Methods: Reverse Osmosis (most common), Electrodialysis, Multi-stage Flash Distillation (MSF), Electrodialysis Reversal (EDR)

⚡Electrodialysis (ED)

Electrodialysis uses electric current and ion-selective membranes to separate dissolved ions from water.

Principle:

When a DC electric current is applied across an electrolyte solution separated by alternating cation exchange membranes (CEM) and anion exchange membranes (AEM):

  • Cations (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) migrate toward cathode (–) through CEM
  • Anions (Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻) migrate toward anode (+) through AEM
  • Ions are removed from central compartments → pure water collected

|

|

|

Membranes:

✅ Advantages of ED
  • Effective for brackish water (low-moderate salinity)
  • Long membrane life
  • Can be reversed (EDR) to reduce scaling
  • Less sensitive to pressure changes
  • Energy efficient for low-salinity water
❌ Limitations
  • Not efficient for very high salinity (seawater)
  • Does NOT remove non-ionic impurities
  • Requires electricity
  • Membranes can foul
  • Higher cost than RO for seawater
📌 Exam Tip: Electrodialysis uses DC current + ion-selective membranes. Best for BRACKISH water (not high-salinity seawater). Cations move to cathode; Anions move to anode!
Defluoridation & Nalgonda Technique
Fluoride problem and removal methods

🦷Fluoride in Water – The Problem

Fluoride (F⁻) is naturally present in groundwater from geological formations. In the right amount, it prevents tooth decay, but excess causes serious health problems.

Fluoride LevelEffect
< 0.5 mg/LDental caries (tooth decay)
0.5 – 1.5 mg/LBeneficial – prevents dental caries (OPTIMAL)
1.5 – 4.0 mg/LDental Fluorosis – mottled/brown teeth
> 4.0 mg/LSkeletal Fluorosis – bone deformity, crippling
> 10 mg/LSevere bone and neurological damage
WHO & BIS limit for fluoride in drinking water: 1.5 mg/L
India problem: Fluorosis is endemic in Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Rajasthan, Gujarat — over 60 million people affected.
📌 Exam Tip: Optimal fluoride = 1 mg/L (prevents caries). Max limit = 1.5 mg/L (BIS/WHO). Excess → Dental fluorosis → Skeletal fluorosis. Frequent exam topic!

🔬Defluoridation Methods

1. Activated Alumina Process

Water is passed through a bed of activated alumina (Al₂O₃). Fluoride ions are adsorbed onto alumina surface.

Al₂O₃ + 6F⁻ → 2AlF₃ + 3O²⁻

Regeneration: using NaOH followed by H₂SO₄ treatment.

2. Bone Char Process

Granular bone charcoal (hydroxyapatite) adsorbs fluoride. Less used now due to social/religious concerns.

3. Ion Exchange

Anion exchange resins exchange OH⁻ for F⁻. Effective but expensive.

4. Coagulation-Precipitation

Using lime or alum. Forms insoluble fluoride compounds that precipitate.

🏆Nalgonda Technique

The Nalgonda Technique is an indigenous (Indian) defluoridation method developed by the National Environmental Engineering Research Institute (NEERI), Nagpur. Named after Nalgonda district in Telangana, which has severe fluorosis problems.

Developed by: NEERI (National Environmental Engineering Research Institute), Nagpur
Named after: Nalgonda district, Telangana – severely affected by fluorosis

Principle:

Based on coagulation, flocculation, and sedimentation using alum and lime.

Mechanism:

Alum hydrolyses to form Al(OH)₃ (gelatinous floc). Fluoride ions are adsorbed and co-precipitated with Al(OH)₃ floc. Lime adjusts pH and aids precipitation. The floc settles, carrying fluoride with it.

Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 6H₂O → 2Al(OH)₃↓ + 3H₂SO₄ F⁻ ions adsorbed on Al(OH)₃ floc → settle down

✅ Advantages of Nalgonda
  • Simple, low-cost technology
  • Locally available chemicals (alum, lime)
  • Suitable for rural/community level
  • No electricity required
  • Can be done at household level (bucket method)
  • Developed in India, for Indian conditions
❌ Limitations
  • High alum dose leaves residual aluminium
  • Produces large volume of sludge
  • Cannot reduce fluoride below 1 mg/L consistently
  • Requires skilled operation for proper dosing
  • Treated water has higher TDS
📌 Exam Tip: Nalgonda Technique = developed by NEERI, uses Alum + Lime, named after Nalgonda district Telangana. Simple, low-cost, no electricity. Very important for TS ECET being a Telangana exam!
⚠️ Key fact: Nalgonda technique does NOT use any special equipment — it can even be done in a bucket at household level (bucket method). This makes it unique and suitable for rural India.
