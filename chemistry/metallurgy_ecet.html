Characteristics of Metals vs Non-Metals
Properties that distinguish metals from non-metals — high-yield topic
Properties of Metals
PropertyDetails
LustreShiny surface; reflect light (metallic lustre)
MalleabilityCan be beaten into thin sheets (e.g., gold foil)
DuctilityCan be drawn into thin wires (copper wire)
ConductivityGood conductors of heat and electricity
HardnessGenerally hard (except Na, K which are soft)
StateSolid at room temperature (except Hg — liquid)
DensityHigh density (except Na, K, Li — float on water)
Melting/Boiling PtGenerally high (except Ga, Cs, Hg)
SoundSonorous — produce ringing sound when struck
OxidesForm basic oxides (e.g., CaO, MgO, Fe₂O₃)
Properties of Non-Metals
PropertyDetails
LustreDull surface (except Iodine — shiny)
MalleabilityBrittle — break when hammered
DuctilityNot ductile
ConductivityPoor conductors (except Graphite)
StateSolid, liquid, or gas at room temperature
DensityGenerally low density
Melting/Boiling PtGenerally low (except Diamond, Graphite)

🎯 Exam Exceptions to Remember:
• Mercury (Hg) = metal, but liquid at room temperature
• Graphite = non-metal, but good conductor of electricity
• Iodine = non-metal, but has metallic lustre
• Diamond = non-metal with very high melting point
• Sodium (Na), Potassium (K) = metals but very soft and low density

Mineral, Ore, Gangue, Flux & Slag
Definitions and differences — most frequently tested as 1-mark MCQs
Key Definitions
TermDefinitionExample
Mineral Natural substance in Earth's crust that contains a metal in its combined form (compound). Every ore is a mineral, but NOT every mineral is an ore. Haematite, Pyrite, Calcite
Ore A mineral from which a metal can be extracted profitably and conveniently. It must have sufficient metal content. Haematite (Fe₂O₃) for Fe; Bauxite (Al₂O₃) for Al
Gangue (Matrix) Unwanted earthy and rocky impurities associated with the ore. Removed during concentration. Sand, clay, stone particles
Flux A substance added during smelting to remove gangue by reacting with it to form a fusible slag. CaO (limestone) — basic flux; SiO₂ — acidic flux
Slag Fusible product formed when flux reacts with gangue. It floats on top of molten metal and is removed. CaSiO₃ (flux CaO + gangue SiO₂)
Flux — Acidic vs Basic
Type of FluxWhen UsedExample
Basic FluxWhen gangue is acidic (SiO₂)CaO (limestone, CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂)
Acidic FluxWhen gangue is basic (FeO, CaO)SiO₂ (silica sand)
Reaction to know: CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (Calcium silicate = slag)
This slag floats → easily separated from molten metal
🎯 MCQ Trap: Pyrite (FeS₂) is a mineral of iron but NOT an ore because extracting iron from it is not economical. Haematite (Fe₂O₃) is the ore of iron.
03
Concentration of Ore
Removing gangue before extraction — know which method for which ore
What is Concentration?

The process of removing gangue (impurities) from the ore to increase the percentage of metal content is called concentration, dressing, or beneficiation.

Methods of Concentration
1
Gravity Separation (Levigation / Hydraulic Washing)
Based on difference in specific gravity (density) of ore and gangue.
Crushed ore is washed with a current of water — lighter gangue is washed away, heavier ore settles.
Used for: Haematite, tinstone (cassiterite), native gold
2
Froth Floatation Process
Based on preferential wetting of ore particles by pine oil. Ore is mixed with water + pine oil + frothing agent + depressant. Air is blown → ore rises with froth, gangue settles.
Used for: Sulphide ores (ZnS, Cu₂S, PbS). Most important method for copper, zinc, lead ores.
3
Magnetic Separation
Based on difference in magnetic properties. The ore or gangue must be magnetic.
Ore passed over a magnetic roller — magnetic part is attracted, non-magnetic part falls away.
Used for: Magnetite (Fe₃O₄), cassiterite (tin ore), chromite
4
Chemical Methods (Leaching)
Ore is dissolved in a suitable chemical solvent; gangue is insoluble and filtered off.
Used for: Bauxite (Al₂O₃) — treated with NaOH (Baeyer's Process). Silver ore — treated with NaCN solution.
5
Electrostatic Separation
Based on difference in electrical conductivity of ore and gangue particles.
Used for: Rutile (TiO₂), zircon — to separate conducting and non-conducting minerals.
⚡ Froth Floatation — Key Chemicals
Pine oil = collector (makes ore surface oily)
Cresol/terpene = frother (creates stable froth)
NaCN or Na₂SiO₃ = depressant (prevents certain minerals from rising)

Reverberatory Furnace
Construction, operation and uses — diagram-based questions common
What is a Reverberatory Furnace?

A furnace in which the fuel and the ore/charge are kept in separate chambers, so they do not come in direct contact. Heat is reflected (reverberated) from the arched roof onto the ore. Used for smelting and refining metals.

Construction
  • Consists of a fire grate (combustion chamber) at one end
  • A low bridge wall separates the fireplace from the hearth
  • The hearth (shallow basin) holds the ore/charge in the middle
  • An arched/sloped roof reverberates (reflects) heat onto the charge
  • A chimney at the far end draws gases out
  • Working doors on the sides for charging and removing materials
  • Built with silica bricks (refractory material)
Operation
1
Fuel (coal/oil/gas) is burned in the fireplace. Hot gases and flames travel over the bridge wall.
2
Hot gases pass over the arched roof → heat is reflected (reverberated) down onto the hearth.
3
The charge (ore + flux) is melted. Metal and slag separate — metal sinks, slag floats.
4
Slag is removed through the slag hole. Metal is tapped through the tap hole at the bottom.
🎯 Used for: Smelting of copper ores, tin, lead, and refining of metals (especially copper matte → blister copper). The fuel and charge do not come in contact — this is the KEY advantage.
05
Blast Furnace
Extraction of iron — most important furnace in TS ECET
Raw Materials (Charge)
  • Haematite (Fe₂O₃) — the ore of iron
  • Coke (C) — fuel + reducing agent
  • Limestone (CaCO₃) — flux
Reactions in Different Zones
ZoneTempKey Reactions
Combustion Zone (Hearth) 1500–1600°C C + O₂ → CO₂; CO₂ + C → 2CO (reducing gas formed)
Reduction Zone (Bosh) 800–1000°C Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ (iron reduced); FeO + C → Fe + CO
Slag Formation (Stack) 400–700°C CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag)
Throat (top) 300–400°C Preheating zone; CO reduces iron oxides partially
🎯 Products: Pig iron (molten iron containing ~4% C, Mn, Si, P, S impurities) collects at the bottom. Slag (CaSiO₃) floats on top. Gases (CO, CO₂, N₂) exit from top.

Pig iron → Cast iron → Wrought iron → Steel (in order of decreasing carbon content)

Extraction Methods: Roasting, Calcination, Smelting
Thermal treatment processes — distinguish clearly for MCQs
1. Roasting
  • Definition: Heating the concentrated ore strongly in the presence of excess air/oxygen below its melting point.
  • Ore type: Used for sulphide ores
  • Products: Metallic oxide + SO₂ (gas is removed)
  • Example: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂↑ (zinc blende roasted)
  • Example: 2PbS + 3O₂ → 2PbO + 2SO₂↑ (galena roasted)
  • Purpose: Converts sulphide → oxide (easier to reduce)
2. Calcination
  • Definition: Heating the concentrated ore strongly in the absence of air (or limited air) below its melting point.
  • Ore type: Used for carbonate and hydrated ores
  • Products: Metallic oxide + CO₂ or H₂O (gases expelled)
  • Example: ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂↑ (calamine calcined)
  • Example: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂↑ (limestone calcined)
  • Example: Al₂O₃·2H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 2H₂O↑ (bauxite calcined)
  • Purpose: Removes CO₂ or moisture; produces porous oxide
3. Smelting
  • Definition: Heating the roasted/calcined ore with a reducing agent (coke) and a flux in a furnace to extract the crude metal.
  • Purpose: Reduces metallic oxide → metal
  • Products: Crude metal + slag
  • Example (Iron): Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂
  • Example (Zinc): 2ZnO + C → 2Zn + CO₂
  • Reducing agents used: Coke (C), CO, Al (thermite process), H₂
ProcessAir SupplyOre TypeGas Removed
RoastingExcess air (O₂)Sulphide oresSO₂
CalcinationNo air / limitedCarbonate/Hydrated oresCO₂ / H₂O
SmeltingWith reducing agent + fluxOxide oresCO₂ (from coke)
⚡ Memory Aid
R-C-S = "Really Careful Smelting"
Roasting = air ON (sulphide) | Calcination = air OFF (carbonate) | Smelting = reduce with coke

Alloys — Composition & Uses
Most direct MCQ topic — memorise composition percentages
What is an Alloy? Purpose of Making Alloys

An alloy is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals (or a metal + non-metal) obtained by melting them together.

Purposes of making alloys:
① To increase hardness (pure metals are too soft — e.g., pure gold/silver too soft for jewelry)
② To improve corrosion resistance (e.g., stainless steel resists rust)
③ To improve tensile strength
④ To lower melting point (solder = Pb+Sn melts easily)
⑤ To improve appearance / lustre
⑥ To change electrical resistance (nichrome for heaters)
🔶 Brass
Composition: Cu 70% + Zn 30%
  • Utensils, decorative items
  • Musical instruments (trumpets)
  • Screws, bolts, locks
  • Electrical fittings
⚪ German Silver
Composition: Cu 60% + Zn 20% + Ni 20%
  • Cutlery, utensils, ornaments
  • Looks like silver — cheaper
  • No actual silver content!
  • Electroplating base
🔴 Nichrome
Composition: Ni 80% + Cr 20%
  • Heating elements (electric heaters, toasters, hair dryers)
  • High resistivity + high melting point
  • Does NOT oxidise at high temp
🔵 Stainless Steel
Composition: Fe 74% + Cr 18% + Ni 8%
  • Utensils, surgical instruments
  • Cutlery, kitchen equipment
  • Resists corrosion (no rust)
  • Decorative structures
🟢 Duralumin
Composition: Al 95% + Cu 4% + Mg 0.5% + Mn 0.5%
  • Aircraft bodies (light + strong)
  • Spacecraft, helicopter parts
  • Pressure cookers, helmets
  • Low density, high strength
