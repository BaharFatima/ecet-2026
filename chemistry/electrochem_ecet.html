Conductors, Insulators & Electrolytes

Types of Substances Based on Conductivity

TypeConductivityCharge CarrierExamples
Conductors High Free electrons Cu, Fe, Al, Graphite
Insulators Nil / Very low None Glass, Rubber, Plastic, Wood
Electrolytes Moderate (in solution/melt) Ions NaCl, HCl, H₂SO₄, NaOH
★ KEY EXAM POINT: Conductors carry electricity by ELECTRONS. Electrolytes carry electricity by IONS. This is the most frequently tested distinction.

Types of Electrolytes

TypeDegree of IonizationExamples
Strong Electrolytes Nearly 100% ionized HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, NaOH, KOH, NaCl, KCl, NaNO₃
Weak Electrolytes Partially ionized (<5%) CH₃COOH (Acetic acid), NH₄OH, H₂CO₃, HCN
Non-Electrolytes 0% — do not ionize Sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁), Urea, Glucose, Alcohol
★ Non-electrolytes do NOT conduct electricity even in solution. Sugar solution = non-conductor.

More Definitions You Must Know

  • Ionization / Dissociation: Breaking of electrolyte into ions when dissolved in water
  • Degree of Ionization (α): Fraction of total electrolyte molecules that are ionized. Strong = α≈1, Weak = α≪1
  • Electrolytic Solution: Solution containing free ions that can conduct electricity
  • Electrodes: Conductors through which current enters/exits the electrolyte
  • Anode (+): Electrode connected to positive terminal — oxidation occurs
  • Cathode (−): Electrode connected to negative terminal — reduction occurs
★ MEMORY TRICK: "An OX and a RED CAT" → ANode = OXidation | CAThode = REDuction

Arrhenius Theory of Electrolytic Dissociation

Postulates of Arrhenius Theory (1884)

  • When an electrolyte is dissolved in water, it spontaneously dissociates (splits) into positively and negatively charged ions
  • The ions are in a state of random motion in solution
  • The process is reversible — ions can recombine (re-association)
  • Solutions of electrolytes are electrically neutral overall (equal positive and negative charges)
  • The extent of dissociation depends on: nature of electrolyte, concentration, and temperature
  • On passing electric current, cations migrate to cathode and anions migrate to anode
★ KEY: Dissociation occurs on DISSOLVING in water — NOT when current is passed. Current only separates them.

Dissociation Examples

NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻
HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻
H₂SO₄ → 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻
NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻
CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ (partial — weak electrolyte)

Effect of Dilution on Conductivity

  • Dilution increases degree of ionization for weak electrolytes
  • For strong electrolytes — already fully ionized, dilution barely affects degree of ionization
  • Specific conductance (κ) decreases on dilution (fewer ions per unit volume)
  • Equivalent / Molar conductance (Λ) increases on dilution
★ MCQ TRAP: Specific conductance DECREASES with dilution but molar conductance INCREASES. These are commonly confused!

Limitations of Arrhenius Theory

  • Does not explain behaviour of strong electrolytes accurately
  • Does not account for ionic interactions in concentrated solutions
  • Fails to explain behaviour in non-aqueous solvents

Electrolysis

What is Electrolysis?

Electrolysis is the process of decomposing a chemical compound using electrical energy. When electric current is passed through an electrolyte (in molten or aqueous state), chemical reactions occur at the electrodes.

Key Components of an Electrolytic Cell

ComponentFunction
Electrolytic CellContainer holding the electrolyte solution/melt
External Battery/DC SourceProvides electrical energy; forces non-spontaneous reaction
Anode (+ve)Oxidation occurs — anions lose electrons here
Cathode (−ve)Reduction occurs — cations gain electrons here
ElectrolyteConducts current via ions; undergoes decomposition
★ Electrolysis = Electrical energy → Chemical energy (opposite of galvanic/voltaic cell)

What Happens at Each Electrode?

  • At Cathode (−): Positive ions (cations like Na⁺, Cu²⁺, H⁺) migrate → gain electrons → reduced → deposited or liberated as gas
  • At Anode (+): Negative ions (anions like Cl⁻, OH⁻, SO₄²⁻) migrate → lose electrons → oxidized → gas liberated or electrode dissolves

Selective Discharge — Preferential Discharge

When multiple ions are present, the ion that is discharged preferentially depends on:

  • Position in electrochemical series — ions lower down (more noble) are discharged first at cathode
  • Concentration of ions — higher concentration ion is preferred
  • Nature of electrode — inert (Pt, C) vs. active (Cu, Ag)
★ At cathode: Cu²⁺ is preferentially discharged over H⁺ (Cu is below H in activity series)
★ At anode: Cl⁻ is preferentially discharged over OH⁻ when conc. of Cl⁻ is high

Applications of Electrolysis

Electrolysis of NaCl — Fused vs Aqueous

① Electrolysis of Fused (Molten) NaCl

NaCl is melted at ~800°C. In fused state, ions are free to move.

NaCl(l) → Na⁺ + Cl⁻
ElectrodeIonReactionProduct
Cathode (−) Na⁺ Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (Reduction) Sodium metal
Anode (+) Cl⁻ 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ (Oxidation) Chlorine gas
★ Fused NaCl → Na metal at cathode + Cl₂ gas at anode. Used industrially to manufacture sodium metal (Down's cell).

② Electrolysis of Aqueous NaCl (Brine)

In aqueous solution, both Na⁺/Cl⁻ ions AND water molecules (H⁺/OH⁻) are present. Selective discharge determines products:

ElectrodeIons PresentDischarged IonReactionProduct
Cathode (−) Na⁺, H⁺ H⁺ (easier to reduce) 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ Hydrogen gas
Anode (+) Cl⁻, OH⁻ Cl⁻ (high conc.) 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ Chlorine gas
★ Remaining solution → NaOH (sodium hydroxide). This is the CHLOR-ALKALI PROCESS — most important industrial electrolysis.

Comparison: Fused NaCl vs Aqueous NaCl

FeatureFused NaClAqueous NaCl

★ MOST IMPORTANT MCQ: Aqueous NaCl gives H₂ at cathode (NOT sodium), because H⁺ from water is more easily reduced than Na⁺.

Chlor-Alkali Products and Uses

  • Cl₂ (Chlorine): Used in PVC, disinfection, bleaching powder, pesticides
  • H₂ (Hydrogen): Used in hydrogenation of oils, fuel, ammonia synthesis
  • NaOH (Caustic soda): Used in soap making, paper industry, textiles, drain cleaners

Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis

First Law of Electrolysis

The mass of substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity (charge) passed through the electrolyte.

m ∝ Q   →   m = Z × Q   →   m = Z × I × t
SymbolQuantityUnit
mMass deposited/liberatedgrams (g)
QQuantity of electricity (charge)Coulombs (C)
ICurrentAmperes (A)
tTimeSeconds (s)
ZElectrochemical equivalentg/C or g/A·s

Electrochemical Equivalent (Z)

Mass deposited per unit charge (per coulomb). Z is characteristic of the element.

Z = E / 96500   where E = Equivalent weight

Second Law of Electrolysis

When the same quantity of electricity passes through different electrolytes, the masses of substances deposited are proportional to their equivalent weights.

m₁/m₂ = E₁/E₂
Equivalent Weight (E) = Atomic Weight / Valency
★ 1 Faraday = 96500 Coulombs = charge of 1 mole of electrons = 6.022 × 10²³ electrons
★ 1 Faraday deposits 1 gram equivalent of any substance

Combined Formula — Most Used in Numericals

m = (E × I × t) / 96500

Where E = Equivalent weight = At. wt / Valency

Numerical Problems — Step by Step

Click "Show Solution" to reveal the step-by-step working.

Problem 01 — First Law
Given: I = 2A, t = 30 min, Cu electrode (E = 32)
Find the mass of copper deposited during electrolysis of CuSO₄ solution.
Convert time: t = 30 × 60 = 1800 seconds
Use formula: m = (E × I × t) / 96500
m = (32 × 2 × 1800) / 96500
m = 115200 / 96500
m ≈ 1.194 g of copper is deposited
Equivalent weight of Cu = 64/2 = 32 (valency of Cu in CuSO₄ = 2)
Problem 02 — Second Law
Same charge passed through CuSO₄ and AgNO₃ solutions. 5.4 g of Ag is deposited.
Find the mass of Cu deposited. (E of Ag = 108, E of Cu = 32)
By Faraday's Second Law: m₁/m₂ = E₁/E₂
m(Cu) / m(Ag) = E(Cu) / E(Ag)
m(Cu) / 5.4 = 32 / 108
m(Cu) = 5.4 × (32/108) = 5.4 × 0.2963
m(Cu) = 1.6 g
Problem 03 — Finding Time
E(Cu) = 32, I = 5A, m = 3.2 g of Cu to be deposited
How long should the current be passed?
Formula: m = (E × I × t) / 96500
Rearrange: t = (m × 96500) / (E × I)
t = (3.2 × 96500) / (32 × 5)
t = 308800 / 160 = 1930 seconds
t = 1930 s ≈ 32.2 minutes
Problem 04 — Faraday Units
2 Faradays of charge passed through molten AlCl₃
Find the mass of Al deposited. (At. wt of Al = 27, valency = 3, E = 9)
1 Faraday deposits 1 gram-equivalent of substance
2 Faradays deposits 2 gram-equivalents of Al
Equivalent weight of Al = 27/3 = 9 g/equiv
Mass = 2 × 9 = 18 g of Al
Shortcut: n Faradays × Equivalent weight = mass deposited
Problem 05 — Finding Electrochemical Equivalent (Z)
2g of a metal deposited when 2A current is passed for 1000 seconds
Calculate the electrochemical equivalent Z of the metal.
From First Law: m = Z × I × t
Z = m / (I × t)
Z = 2 / (2 × 1000)
Z = 0.001 g/C = 1 × 10⁻³ g/C
Problem 06 — Volume of Gas
Current = 1A passed through H₂SO₄ solution for 2 hours. E(H) = 1, E(O) = 8
Calculate the mass of H₂ liberated at cathode.
t = 2 × 3600 = 7200 s
m(H₂) = (E × I × t) / 96500
Note: For H₂, use E = 1 (for H atom, not H₂ molecule)
m = (1 × 1 × 7200) / 96500
m ≈ 0.0746 g of H₂
