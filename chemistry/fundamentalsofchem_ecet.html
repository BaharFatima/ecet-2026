Atomic Structure
▾
Atomic Number & Mass Number
Atomic Number (Z) = number of protons in the nucleus. It defines the element identity.
Mass Number (A) = number of protons + neutrons. Number of neutrons = A − Z.
Z = number of protons = number of electrons (neutral atom)
A = Z + N   |   N (neutrons) = A − Z
Example: ⁵⁶Fe → Z = 26 (protons), A = 56, N = 30
Isotopes & Isobars

Isotopes

Same Z, different A (same element, different neutrons).
Example: ¹H, ²H (deuterium), ³H (tritium) — all hydrogen.

Isobars

Same A, different Z (different elements, same mass number).
Example: ⁴⁰Ca (Z=20) and ⁴⁰Ar (Z=18).
⚠️ MCQ Trick: Isotopes → same Z (same element). Isobars → same A (different element).
Bohr's Atomic Theory
Bohr proposed (1913) that electrons revolve in fixed circular orbits (shells) around the nucleus without radiating energy. Key postulates:
1. Electrons orbit in specific stationary states (shells: K, L, M, N…).
2. Each shell has a fixed energy. Electrons emit/absorb energy only when jumping between shells.
3. Angular momentum is quantised: mvr = nh/2π
Energy of emitted photon: E = E₂ − E₁ = hν
Limitation: Cannot explain spectra of multi-electron atoms or fine structure.

An orbital is a three-dimensional region where probability of finding an electron is ≥ 90%. Unlike Bohr's orbit, orbitals have definite shapes.

Aufbau Principle
Electrons fill orbitals in order of increasing energy (n+l rule).
Order: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p…
n+l rule: Lower (n+l) → filled first. For equal (n+l) → lower n first.
Example: 3d has n+l = 3+2 = 5; 4s has n+l = 4+0 = 4 → 4s fills before 3d.
Hund's Rule of Maximum Multiplicity
In degenerate orbitals (orbitals of equal energy), electrons are distributed singly with parallel spins before pairing begins.
Example: Nitrogen (7e⁻): 1s² 2s² 2px¹ 2py¹ 2pz¹ — all three 2p electrons are unpaired.
⚠️ MCQ favourite: Carbon (C) has 2 unpaired electrons; Nitrogen (N) has 3 unpaired electrons.
Pauli's Exclusion Principle
No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers (n, l, ml, ms). An orbital can hold at most 2 electrons with opposite spins (↑↓).
Quantum numbers: n (principal), l (azimuthal/subsidiary), ml (magnetic), ms (spin ±½)
Consequence: Each subshell capacity → s=2, p=6, d=10, f=14

Chemical Bonding
▾
Electronic Theory of Valency (Lewis)
G.N. Lewis proposed that atoms form bonds by sharing or transferring electrons to achieve a stable octet (8 electrons in outermost shell) or duplet (H, He with 2 electrons). Valence electrons (outermost shell e⁻) participate in bonding.
Types of Chemical Bonds

Ionic Bond (Electrovalent)

Complete transfer of electrons. Formed between metals and non-metals. Example: NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻. High melting point, conducts electricity in molten/solution form.

Covalent Bond

Mutual sharing of electron pairs. Formed between non-metals. Example: H₂, Cl₂, H₂O. Low melting point, poor conductor.

Coordinate Covalent Bond

Both electrons donated by one atom (donor = Lewis base). Example: NH₃ → BF₃ (N donates lone pair to B). Also called dative bond.

Metallic Bond

Sea of delocalised electrons among metal cations lattice. Explains conductivity, malleability, ductility. Example: Cu, Fe, Al.
Hydrogen Bond
Weak electrostatic attraction between H (covalently bonded to F, O, or N) and a lone pair on F, O, or N of another molecule.

Intermolecular H-bond

Between different molecules. Example: H₂O, HF, NH₃, alcohols. Causes high boiling point and surface tension.

Intramolecular H-bond

Within same molecule (chelation). Example: o-nitrophenol, salicylaldehyde. Causes lower boiling point than intermolecular.
Effect on physical properties:
• High boiling point: H₂O (100°C) vs H₂S (−60°C) — due to intermolecular H-bonds in water
• High viscosity and surface tension in water
• Anomalous expansion of water at 4°C (H-bond network)
⚠️ Strongest H-bond: F–H···F. Order: F > O > N

Oxidation – Reduction (Redox)
▾
Electronic Concept

Oxidation

Loss of electrons (OIL — Oxidation Is Loss). Increase in oxidation number. The substance oxidised is the reducing agent.

Reduction

Gain of electrons (RIG — Reduction Is Gain). Decrease in oxidation number. The substance reduced is the oxidising agent.
Memory: OIL RIG → Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain
Oxidation Number Rules
1. Free element → oxidation number = 0 (e.g., O₂, Fe, H₂ all = 0)
2. Monatomic ion → oxidation number = charge (e.g., Fe³⁺ = +3)
3. In compounds: O is usually −2 (except in peroxides: −1, and OF₂: +2)
4. H is usually +1 (except in metal hydrides: −1, e.g., NaH)
5. F is always −1 in compounds (most electronegative)
6. Alkali metals (Group 1) = +1; Alkaline earth (Group 2) = +2
7. Sum of oxidation numbers = 0 for neutral compound; = charge for polyatomic ion
⚠️ Exceptions to remember:
• O in H₂O₂ = −1 | O in OF₂ = +2
• H in NaH, CaH₂ = −1
• Mn in KMnO₄ = +7 | Cr in K₂Cr₂O₇ = +6
Calculating Oxidation Numbers — Step by Step
Example 1: Find oxidation number (ON) of S in H₂SO₄
H = +1 (×2 = +2) | O = −2 (×4 = −8) | Let S = x
+2 + x + (−8) = 0 → x = +6 ✓

Example 2: ON of Cr in Cr₂O₇²⁻
Let Cr = x: 2x + 7(−2) = −2 → 2x − 14 = −2 → x = +6 ✓

Example 3: ON of Fe in Fe₃O₄
3x + 4(−2) = 0 → 3x = +8 → x = +8/3 (mixed: +2 and +3)
Identifying Oxidising & Reducing Agents
In the reaction: Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu

• Zn → Zn²⁺: loses 2e⁻ → oxidised → Zn is the reducing agent
• Cu²⁺ → Cu: gains 2e⁻ → reduced → Cu²⁺ (CuSO₄) is the oxidising agent
⚠️ Common oxidising agents: KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇, HNO₃, H₂O₂, O₃
Common reducing agents: H₂, CO, SO₂, Fe²⁺, C, Na
Disproportionation
A reaction where the same element is simultaneously oxidised and reduced.
Example: Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O
Cl₂ (0) → NaCl (−1, reduced) and NaOCl (+1, oxidised)
