📌 Physical Quantities & SI Units

A physical quantity is anything that can be measured. They are of two types: Fundamental (Base) quantities — independent, not derived from others; and Derived quantities — obtained by combining fundamental ones (e.g., velocity = distance/time).

SI Base QuantityUnitSymbol
Lengthmetrem
Masskilogramkg
Timeseconds
Electric CurrentampereA
TemperaturekelvinK
Amount of substancemolemol
Luminous intensitycandelacd

Advantages of SI: Internationally accepted, coherent (derived units obtained by simple multiplication/division with no conversion factors), rational (one unit per physical quantity), decimal-based.

📌 Dimensions & Dimensional Analysis

The dimension of a physical quantity tells you which fundamental quantities it is made of and in what power. We write dimensions using letters: M(mass), L(length), T(time), I(current), θ(temperature).

Important Dimensional Formulae (Memorize These)
Velocity = [LT⁻¹] Acceleration = [LT⁻²] Force (F=ma) = [MLT⁻²] Work/Energy = [ML²T⁻²] Power = [ML²T⁻³] Pressure = [ML⁻¹T⁻²] Momentum = [MLT⁻¹] Impulse = [MLT⁻¹] Torque = [ML²T⁻²] Gravitational Const G = [M⁻¹L³T⁻²] Planck's Const h = [ML²T⁻¹] Density = [ML⁻³] Frequency = [T⁻¹] Angle = Dimensionless [M⁰L⁰T⁰]

Principle of Homogeneity: Every term in a physically correct equation must have the same dimensions. This is used to: (1) check correctness of equations, (2) derive relations between quantities, (3) convert units from one system to another.

Checking Equation Correctness

Check v² = u² + 2as LHS: [v²] = [LT⁻¹]² = [L²T⁻²] RHS: [u²] = [L²T⁻²]; [2as] = [LT⁻²·L] = [L²T⁻²] All terms = [L²T⁻²] ✓ Dimensionally correct!

📌 Scalars vs Vectors

Scalar: Has only magnitude. Examples: mass, temperature, speed, distance, energy, time.
Vector: Has both magnitude AND direction. Examples: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, momentum. Represented by arrow over letter (→A) or bold (A). The magnitude of vector A is |A| or simply A.

Vector TypeDescription
Unit Vector (â)Magnitude = 1; used to show direction only. â = A/|A|
Null/Zero VectorMagnitude = 0, direction undefined. A+0=A
Equal VectorsSame magnitude AND same direction
Negative VectorSame magnitude, opposite direction: −A
Position VectorVector from origin O to point P: OP = r = xî+yĵ+zk̂

📌 Resolution of Vectors

Any vector A making angle θ with x-axis can be split into: Aₓ = A cosθ (horizontal component) and Aᵧ = A sinθ (vertical component). In 3D: A = Aₓî + Aᵧĵ + Aᵤk̂, magnitude = √(Aₓ²+Aᵧ²+Aᵤ²).

📌 Parallelogram Law of Vector Addition

Statement: If two vectors A and B act simultaneously on a particle represented as adjacent sides of a parallelogram, then the resultant is the diagonal of the parallelogram.

Resultant Magnitude and Direction
R = √(A² + B² + 2AB·cosθ) where θ = angle between A and B Direction of resultant (angle α with A): tan α = B·sinθ / (A + B·cosθ) Special cases: θ=0° (same direction): R = A+B (maximum resultant) θ=180° (opposite): R = |A−B| (minimum resultant) θ=90°: R = √(A²+B²)

📌 Scalar (Dot) Product

A·B = |A||B|cosθ. Result is a scalar. Geometrically it is the projection of one vector on the other times the magnitude of the other.

î·î = ĵ·ĵ = k̂·k̂ = 1 (parallel unit vectors) î·ĵ = ĵ·k̂ = k̂·î = 0 (perpendicular unit vectors) A·B = AₓBₓ + AᵧBᵧ + AᵤBᵤ (component form) Commutative: A·B = B·A Distributive: A·(B+C) = A·B + A·C If A⊥B: A·B = 0 Applications: Work: W = F·d = Fd·cosθ Power: P = F·v

📌 Vector (Cross) Product

A×B = |A||B|sinθ·n̂ where n̂ is perpendicular to both A and B (right-hand rule). Result is a vector.

î×î = ĵ×ĵ = k̂×k̂ = 0 î×ĵ = k̂, ĵ×k̂ = î, k̂×î = ĵ (cyclic order) ĵ×î = −k̂ (anti-cyclic = negative) A×B = |î ĵ k̂ | |Aₓ Aᵧ Aᵤ| |Bₓ Bᵧ Bᵤ| NOT commutative: A×B = −(B×A) If A∥B: A×B = 0 Applications: Torque: τ = r×F (magnitude = rF sinθ) Area of parallelogram = |A×B| Area of triangle = (1/2)|A×B|

📌 Lami's Theorem

If three concurrent coplanar forces are in equilibrium, each force divided by the sine of the angle between the other two is constant:

F₁/sin α₁ = F₂/sin α₂ = F₃/sin α₃ where α₁,α₂,α₃ are angles OPPOSITE to F₁,F₂,F₃ respectively.

📌 Friction — Complete Theory

Friction is a contact force that opposes relative motion between surfaces. It acts parallel to the surface. It arises due to intermolecular attractions and interlocking of surface irregularities.

Types and Laws of Friction
Static Friction: opposes impending motion; can vary from 0 to μₛN Limiting Friction (fₗ): maximum static friction = μₛ·N Kinetic Friction: constant once motion begins = μₖ·N (μₖ < μₛ) Normal Reaction: N = mg·cosθ (on incline at angle θ) Coefficient of friction: μ = tan φ where φ = angle of friction (angle of friction = angle R makes with normal) On horizontal surface: Acceleration: a = μg (deceleration while sliding) Distance to stop from speed u: s = u²/(2μg) Time to stop: t = u/(μg) On rough inclined plane (angle θ, block sliding down): a = g(sinθ − μcosθ) [μcosθ = friction component] For equilibrium: tanθ ≤ μ Angle of repose = tan⁻¹(μ) ← angle at which block just begins to slide

📌 Projectile Motion — Full Derivation

A projectile moves under gravity alone (no air resistance). It has: horizontal motion (uniform velocity, no force) and vertical motion (uniformly accelerated by gravity). These two are independent of each other.

Oblique Projection (angle θ, initial speed u)
Horizontal component: uₓ = u cosθ (constant throughout) Vertical component: uᵧ = u sinθ (decreases going up) At time t: x = u cosθ · t y = u sinθ · t − (1/2)g·t² vₓ = u cosθ (unchanged) vᵧ = u sinθ − g·t Key Results: Max Height: H = u²sin²θ / 2g Time of Ascent: t₁ = u sinθ / g Time of Flight: T = 2u sinθ / g (= 2 × ascent time) Horizontal Range: R = u²sin2θ / g Maximum Range: R_max = u²/g (occurs at θ=45°) Equation of Trajectory (parabola): y = x tanθ − gx²/(2u²cos²θ) ← this is a parabola y = ax − bx²
💡

Key insight: Range is same for complementary angles. If θ₁+θ₂=90°, then R(θ₁)=R(θ₂). e.g., range at 30° = range at 60°. Maximum range is always at 45°. For maximum height: θ=90°.

Horizontal Projection (thrown horizontally from height H)
x = u·t (horizontal, uniform) y = (1/2)g·t² (vertical, from rest) Time to reach ground: t = √(2H/g) Range: R = u·√(2H/g) Final velocity: v = √(u²+g²t²), angle = tan⁻¹(gt/u) below horizontal

📌 Circular Motion — Complete Theory

When a particle moves in a circle of radius r, even at constant speed it is accelerating because its direction changes. This acceleration (centripetal) always points toward the center.

Key Quantities and Relations
Angular displacement: θ (in radians) Angular velocity: ω = dθ/dt = 2πf = 2π/T (rad/s) Angular acceleration: α = dω/dt (rad/s²) Linear ↔ Angular: v = rω (linear velocity = radius × angular velocity) a = rα (linear acceleration = radius × angular acceleration) Centripetal (towards centre): aₙ = v²/r = rω² (centripetal acceleration) Fₙ = mv²/r = mrω² (centripetal force — provided by tension, gravity, normal force etc.) Centrifugal force: −mv²/r (pseudo force in rotating frame, appears to act outward) Period: T = 2πr/v = 2π/ω Frequency: f = 1/T
Banking of Roads
A banked road allows vehicles to take a turn without relying on friction. The required banking angle θ: tan θ = v²/rg (ideal banking, no friction) v_optimal = √(rg·tanθ) For a cyclist bending inward: tan θ = v²/rg (same relation — cyclist bends to provide centripetal force)
Numerical Example

A car moves at 72 km/h on a circular track of radius 200 m. Find centripetal acceleration and force (mass=1000 kg). v = 72 km/h = 72×(1000/3600) = 20 m/s aₙ = v²/r = 400/200 = 2 m/s² Fₙ = m·aₙ = 1000×2 = 2000 N

📌 Elasticity — Stress, Strain & Hooke's Law

Elasticity is the property of a material to regain its original shape after the deforming force is removed. Elastic limit — the maximum stress up to which a body is perfectly elastic. Beyond this, permanent deformation occurs.

Stress = Force / Area = F/A Units: Pa (= N/m²) Types: Tensile (stretching), Compressive (squeezing), Shear (sliding) Strain = Change / Original = ΔL/L (dimensionless) Types: Longitudinal (ΔL/L), Volumetric (ΔV/V), Shear strain Hooke's Law: Within elastic limit, Stress ∝ Strain i.e., Stress/Strain = constant = Modulus of Elasticity Young's Modulus (Y): for longitudinal stress/strain Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) = FL/(AΔL) Units: Pa or N/m² Derivation: stretch a wire of length L, area A, by force F, elongation ΔL Bulk Modulus (K): for volumetric stress/strain K = −P/(ΔV/V) (negative because volume decreases under pressure) Modulus of Rigidity (G): for shear stress/strain G = Shear Stress / Shear Strain
💡

Stress-strain curve key points: OA = perfectly elastic (Hooke's law); A = proportional limit; B = elastic limit; C = yield point (permanent deformation starts); D = ultimate stress (max load); E = breaking point (fracture).

📌 Surface Tension & Capillarity

Surface tension (T) is the force per unit length acting along the surface of a liquid, due to intermolecular attractions. The surface behaves like a stretched elastic membrane. Unit: N/m.

Surface tension: T = F/L (force per unit length) Excess pressure inside: Liquid drop: ΔP = 2T/r Soap bubble: ΔP = 4T/r (two surfaces) Air bubble in liquid: ΔP = 2T/r Capillary Rise (angle of contact θ): h = 2T cosθ / (rρg) where h=height, T=surface tension, θ=contact angle, r=radius of capillary, ρ=liquid density, g=9.8 m/s² Effect of temperature: Surface tension DECREASES with increase in temperature. Effect of impurity: Soap/detergent DECREASES surface tension (used for cleaning). Dissolved salt INCREASES surface tension.

📌 Viscosity — Newton's Law & Poiseuille's Equation

Viscosity is the internal friction between layers of fluid in relative motion. A high-viscosity fluid (like honey) flows slowly; low-viscosity (water) flows freely.

Newton's Law of Viscosity: F = η · A · (dv/dx) Viscous force = η × area × velocity gradient η = Coefficient of viscosity Unit: Pa·s (N·s/m²) Poiseuille's Equation (volume flow through a pipe): Q = πr⁴P / (8ηL) where Q=flow rate (m³/s), r=pipe radius, P=pressure diff, L=length Effect of Temperature: Liquids: η DECREASES as T increases (less intermolecular bonding) Gases: η INCREASES as T increases (more kinetic energy → more momentum transfer) This is the OPPOSITE behavior — very commonly tested!

📌 Fluid Dynamics — Bernoulli's Theorem

Streamline flow: orderly, layers don't mix Turbulent flow: chaotic mixing (occurs above critical velocity) Reynolds Number: Re = ρvD/η Re < 2000 → streamline; Re > 3000 → turbulent Equation of Continuity (mass conservation): A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ (for incompressible fluid) Narrow pipe → faster flow; Wide pipe → slower flow Bernoulli's Theorem (energy conservation along streamline): P + (1/2)ρv² + ρgh = constant P = pressure energy per unit volume (1/2)ρv² = kinetic energy per unit volume ρgh = potential energy per unit volume Applications: Venturimeter (measures flow rate using pressure difference) Aerofoil/Aircraft wing (faster flow above = lower pressure → lift) Carburetor (fuel atomization) Spray gun (atomizer)

📌 Work, Energy & Power

Work: W = F·d·cosθ where θ = angle between force and displacement W = 0 if force ⊥ displacement (e.g., circular motion, normal force) W > 0 if force in direction of motion; W < 0 if against motion Unit: Joule (J) = N·m Kinetic Energy: KE = (1/2)mv² [energy of motion] Potential Energy (Gravitational): PE = mgh [energy of position] Power: P = W/t = F·v·cosθ Unit: Watt (W) 1 Horsepower = 746 W 1 kWh = 3.6×10⁶ J (unit of electrical energy)

📌 Work-Energy Theorem

The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy. This is derived directly from Newton's second law integrated over displacement.

Derivation: F = ma, and v² = u² + 2as → as = (v²−u²)/2 W = F·s = ma·s = m·(as) = m·(v²−u²)/2 = (1/2)mv² − (1/2)mu² W_net = ΔKE = KE_final − KE_initial If W_net > 0 → KE increases (speeds up) If W_net < 0 → KE decreases (slows down) If W_net = 0 → KE unchanged (constant speed)

📌 Conservation of Energy

Law of Conservation of Energy: Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be converted from one form to another. The total mechanical energy (KE+PE) of a system is conserved when only conservative forces act.

Proof: Freely Falling Body (mass m, released from height H)
At top (height H): KE=0, PE=mgH, Total=mgH At height h: v²=2g(H−h); KE=(1/2)mv²=mg(H−h); PE=mgh; Total=mgH ✓ At bottom (h=0): v²=2gH; KE=mgH; PE=0; Total=mgH ✓ Total mechanical energy remains mgH throughout!
Simple Pendulum — Energy Transformation
At extreme position (max displacement A): v = 0 → KE = 0 (all PE) PE = mgL(1−cosθ) = max At mean position (equilibrium): PE = 0 (minimum) v = max → KE = max = (1/2)mv²_max At any position x from mean: KE = (1/2)mω²(A²−x²) PE = (1/2)mω²x² Total E = (1/2)mω²A² = constant

📌 Thermal Expansion of Solids

Linear expansion: l = l₀(1 + αΔT), ΔT = T−T₀ Areal expansion: A = A₀(1 + βΔT), β = 2α Volumetric: V = V₀(1 + γΔT), γ = 3α Relation: α : β : γ = 1 : 2 : 3 (Remember: linear→area→volume means ×2, ×3 for the coefficient)

📌 Gas Laws & Ideal Gas Equation

Boyle's Law (T=const): PV = constant → P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ Charles' Law (P=const): V/T = constant → V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ Combined Gas Law: PV/T = constant → P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ Ideal Gas Equation: PV = nRT n = number of moles R = Universal gas constant = 8.314 J/(mol·K) T = Temperature in KELVIN (T = 273 + t°C) In terms of density (ρ = mass/volume): P = ρRT/M where M = molar mass

📌 First Law of Thermodynamics

The first law is simply the law of conservation of energy applied to thermodynamic systems: ΔQ = ΔU + W where ΔQ = heat supplied to system, ΔU = increase in internal energy, W = work done by system.

Work done by gas expanding against pressure P: W = P·ΔV = P(V₂−V₁) (for constant pressure process) W = ∫P dV in general Isothermal Process (T=const, ideal gas → ΔU=0): ΔQ = W → all heat converts to work W = nRT·ln(V₂/V₁) Adiabatic Process (ΔQ=0, no heat exchange): ΔU = −W → work done at expense of internal energy PVᵞ = constant (γ = Cp/Cv = adiabatic index) Temperature drops when gas expands adiabatically Second Law: Heat flows spontaneously from hot to cold (not reverse). Kelvin-Planck: No engine can convert all heat to work. Clausius: Refrigerator needs work input to transfer heat from cold to hot.

📌 SHM — Definition & Conditions

Simple Harmonic Motion is a special type of periodic motion where the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement from equilibrium and directed toward it: F = −kx. The negative sign means the force always opposes displacement.

Defining Equation: a = −ω²x (acceleration ∝ −displacement) ω = angular frequency = 2πf = 2π/T Displacement: x = A·sin(ωt + φ) or x = A·cos(ωt + φ) A = amplitude (maximum displacement from mean position) φ = initial phase (depends on initial conditions) Velocity: v = dx/dt = Aω·cos(ωt + φ) v = ω√(A²−x²) [velocity at any position x] v_max = Aω (at x=0, mean position) Acceleration: a = −ω²x a_max = ω²A (at x=±A, extreme positions) Time Period: T = 2π/ω Frequency: f = 1/T = ω/2π

📌 Simple Pendulum — Complete Derivation

An ideal simple pendulum = point mass m on massless inextensible string of length L, oscillating in a plane. For small angles (θ<5°), sinθ≈θ, which makes the motion SHM.

Restoring force = −mg sinθ ≈ −mgθ = −mg(x/L) = −(mg/L)·x Compare with F = −kx: k = mg/L → ω = √(k/m) = √(g/L) Time Period: T = 2π√(L/g) Laws of Simple Pendulum: 1. T ∝ √L (increase length 4× → period doubles) 2. T ∝ 1/√g (period increases at higher altitudes, lower g) 3. T is INDEPENDENT of mass m 4. T is INDEPENDENT of amplitude (for small oscillations only) Second's Pendulum: T = 2s → L = g/π² ≈ 1 m (at g=9.8 m/s²)
Numerical Example

A pendulum has T=2s at g=9.8 m/s². Find length. T = 2π√(L/g) → 2 = 2π√(L/9.8) → 1/π = √(L/9.8) L = 9.8/π² = 9.8/9.87 ≈ 0.993 m ≈ 1 m

📌 Doppler Effect

The Doppler Effect is the change in observed frequency of a wave when there is relative motion between source and observer. When they approach each other, frequency appears higher (pitch rises); when receding, frequency appears lower.

Apparent frequency: f' = f · (v ± vₒ) / (v ∓ vₛ) v = speed of sound in medium vₒ = speed of observer (+ if moving toward source, − if away) vₛ = speed of source (− if moving toward observer, + if away) Memory tip: "Approaching increases, Receding decreases" When source moves toward stationary observer: f' = fv/(v−vₛ) > f When source moves away: f' = fv/(v+vₛ) < f When observer moves toward stationary source: f' = f(v+vₒ)/v > f Applications: RADAR speed detection, medical ultrasound, astronomy (redshift/blueshift)

📌 Beats, Echo & Reverberation

Beats: superposition of two sound waves of slightly different frequencies Beat frequency = |f₁ − f₂| (must be < ~10 Hz to be heard as beats) Applications: tuning musical instruments Echo: reflection of sound heard distinctly after original sound Minimum distance for echo at 20°C: d = vt/2 = 340×0.1/2 = 17 m (ear needs ≥0.1s separation to distinguish echo from original) Stationary Waves: formed by superposition of two identical waves traveling in opposite directions. Nodes (zero amplitude) and Antinodes (maximum amplitude) are fixed in space. Reverberation: persistence of sound due to multiple reflections Sabine's Formula: T = 0.165V/ΣaS T = reverberation time (seconds) V = volume of room (m³) ΣaS = total absorption = Σ(absorption coefficient × area of each surface) Good auditorium: T = 0.5 to 1.5 seconds Noise pollution control: barriers, absorption panels, plantations

📌 Kirchhoff's Laws

These are the fundamental laws for analyzing any electrical circuit, based on conservation of charge (KCL) and conservation of energy (KVL).

KCL (Kirchhoff's Current Law): At any junction, ΣI_in = ΣI_out i.e., sum of currents entering = sum leaving (conservation of charge) ΣI = 0 at a node KVL (Kirchhoff's Voltage Law): Around any closed loop, ΣV = 0 i.e., sum of all potential differences = 0 (conservation of energy) Sign convention: EMF positive if traversed from − to +; Resistor drop negative if traversed in current direction

📌 Wheatstone Bridge & Meter Bridge

The Wheatstone Bridge is a circuit for precisely measuring unknown resistance. It consists of 4 resistors P, Q, R, S in a diamond pattern with a galvanometer (G) across the middle diagonal.

Balanced Condition: P/Q = R/S When balanced, no current flows through galvanometer (Ig=0) This gives very accurate measurement of unknown resistance. Meter Bridge (Practical Wheatstone Bridge): Uses a 1-metre resistance wire. The balance point (null point) at length l: R/S = l/(100−l) → Unknown S = R(100−l)/l Always: P corresponds to 0-l (length l) Q corresponds to l-100 (length 100−l)

📌 Electromagnetic Induction

Faraday's Discovery: A changing magnetic flux through a circuit induces an EMF in it. This is the basis of generators, transformers, and induction motors.

Magnetic Flux: Φ = B·A·cosθ Units: Weber (Wb) Faraday's Laws: 1st Law: Whenever magnetic flux linked with a circuit changes, an EMF is induced. 2nd Law: Magnitude of induced EMF = rate of change of flux: e = −dΦ/dt (negative sign from Lenz's Law) Lenz's Law: The induced current always flows in a direction to OPPOSE the change that caused it (conservation of energy principle). Self-Inductance (L): When current in a coil changes, it induces EMF in itself. e = −L·dI/dt L = NΦ/I (Henry, H) Mutual Inductance (M): EMF induced in coil 2 due to changing current in coil 1: e₂ = −M·dI₁/dt

📌 Transformer — Principle & Types

Principle: Mutual induction between two coils wound on same iron core. AC input in primary → changing flux → induced EMF in secondary. Transformer Equations: Vₛ/Vₚ = Nₛ/Nₚ = Iₚ/Iₛ (voltage ratio = turns ratio; current ratio is inverse of turns ratio) For ideal transformer: Pₛ = Pₚ → VₛIₛ = VₚIₚ Step-up Transformer: Nₛ > Nₚ → Vₛ > Vₚ (increases voltage, decreases current) Step-down Transformer: Nₛ < Nₚ → Vₛ < Vₚ (decreases voltage, increases current) Efficiency: η = Pₒᵤₜ/Pᵢₙ × 100% (ideal = 100%, real ≈ 95–99%) Losses: Copper loss (I²R in windings), Eddy current loss, Hysteresis loss

📌 Photoelectric Effect — Einstein's Theory

When light falls on a metal surface, electrons are emitted. This cannot be explained by wave theory (wave theory predicts any frequency should work given enough intensity). Einstein used the quantum/particle nature of light (photons) to explain it.

Einstein's Photoelectric Equation: hf = W₀ + KE_max hf = hf₀ + (1/2)mv²_max h = Planck's constant = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J·s f = frequency of incident light W₀ = Work function = hf₀ (minimum energy to eject electron from surface) f₀ = Threshold frequency (minimum frequency for emission) KE_max = (1/2)mv²_max = maximum kinetic energy of ejected electron Stopping Potential V₀: minimum potential to stop fastest photoelectron eV₀ = (1/2)mv²_max = h(f−f₀)
Laws of Photoelectric Effect
1. Emission is INSTANTANEOUS (no time delay) — proves particle nature 2. Maximum KE of emitted electrons depends on FREQUENCY, not intensity 3. There is a minimum threshold frequency f₀ below which NO emission occurs, regardless of intensity 4. Number of photoelectrons emitted per second is proportional to INTENSITY of light 5. Work function W₀ depends on the metal surface, not on incident light

📌 Dual Nature of Light

Light behaves as a wave in phenomena like interference, diffraction, and polarization. It behaves as a particle (photon) in photoelectric effect and Compton effect. This wave-particle duality is a fundamental quantum mechanical concept.

Wave nature evidence: interference (Young's double slit), diffraction, polarization Particle nature evidence: photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, radiation pressure Photon energy: E = hf = hc/λ de Broglie wavelength of matter: λ = h/(mv) = h/p (every particle also has wave nature — dual nature of matter!) Young's Double Slit (interference): Fringe width: β = λD/d λ=wavelength, D=screen distance, d=slit separation Constructive (bright fringe): path diff = nλ Destructive (dark fringe): path diff = (2n+1)λ/2

📌 LASER — Complete Theory

LASER = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. It produces highly coherent, monochromatic, directional, and intense light.

Types of Emission: Spontaneous Emission: atom in excited state spontaneously falls to ground state, emitting a photon randomly (no trigger). Direction and phase are random. Stimulated Emission (Einstein, 1917): an incoming photon of EXACT energy (E₂−E₁) triggers the excited atom to emit a photon of IDENTICAL energy, direction, phase, and polarization. This is what makes LASER coherent and intense. Population Inversion: For LASER, more atoms must be in excited state than ground state — this is population inversion (achieved by pumping: optical, electrical, chemical) Types: Ruby LASER: 3-level system; pulsed; red light (694 nm); used in holography He-Ne LASER: 4-level system; continuous; red (632.8 nm); used in barcode scanners CO₂ LASER: high power; IR; industrial cutting and welding Semiconductor LASER: compact; used in optical fiber communication, DVD players LASER Characteristics: Monochromatic, Coherent (all waves in phase), Directional (very low divergence), Highly Intense (all power in thin beam) Applications: Surgery (ophthalmology), cutting/welding, fiber optic communication, holography, CD/DVD, barcode readers, LIDAR, distance measurement

📌 Optical Fiber — Total Internal Reflection

Optical fibers use Total Internal Reflection (TIR) to guide light along a thin glass/plastic fiber with essentially zero loss.

Total Internal Reflection (TIR): Occurs when: (1) light travels from denser to rarer medium (n₁ > n₂) (2) angle of incidence > critical angle θc Critical angle: sinθc = n₂/n₁ (from Snell's law with r=90°) Optical Fiber Structure: Core: inner glass, high refractive index n₁ (~1.5) Cladding: outer glass/plastic, lower n₂ → ensures TIR at interface Buffer/Jacket: protective coating Types: Single-mode fiber: very thin core (~9μm); one light path; used for long distance Multi-mode fiber: thicker core (~50μm); multiple paths; used for short distance Graded-index: n varies gradually; reduces dispersion Applications: Telecommunication (internet backbone), endoscopy (medical imaging), sensors, local area networks, decorative lighting

📌 Nanotechnology & Superconductivity

Nanotechnology: manipulation of matter at 1–100 nm scale Nanoparticles have enormous surface-area-to-volume ratio → different properties than bulk material (different melting point, optical, magnetic, catalytic properties) Applications: targeted drug delivery, nano-coatings, nano-electronics, stronger materials, cancer treatment, water purification Superconductivity: Some materials have ZERO electrical resistance below a critical temperature Tc (e.g., Mercury: Tc=4.2K; YBCO ceramic: Tc≈93K) Meissner Effect: superconductors expel magnetic field (perfect diamagnet) Applications: MRI machines, maglev trains, particle accelerators (LHC), lossless power transmission

📌 Energy Bands in Solids

In isolated atoms, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. In a solid, atoms are so close that these levels broaden into energy bands due to quantum mechanical interaction. The two critical bands are: Valence Band (filled with valence electrons) and Conduction Band (where electrons can move freely). The Forbidden Gap (Bandgap) between them determines electrical behavior.

Fermi Level: The energy level at which the probability of finding an electron is exactly 50%. In intrinsic semiconductors, Fermi level lies in the middle of the bandgap.

📌 Intrinsic & Extrinsic Semiconductors

Intrinsic Semiconductor (pure): At 0K: no free carriers, acts as insulator. At room temperature: thermal energy breaks covalent bonds. Each broken bond creates one electron (in conduction band) + one hole (in valence band). ne = nh = nᵢ (intrinsic carrier concentration, increases with temperature) Doping: Adding tiny amounts of impurity to drastically increase conductivity. N-type (Donor doping): Pentavalent dopant (5 valence electrons): P, As, Sb, Bi added to Si 4 electrons form covalent bonds; 1 extra electron is "donated" to conduction band Majority carriers: ELECTRONS; Minority: holes Fermi level shifts toward conduction band P-type (Acceptor doping): Trivalent dopant (3 valence electrons): B, Al, Ga, In added to Si 3 electrons form bonds; 1 incomplete bond = HOLE created Majority carriers: HOLES; Minority: electrons Fermi level shifts toward valence band

📌 PN Junction Diode — Complete Theory

When P-type and N-type are joined, electrons from N-side diffuse to P-side and holes from P-side diffuse to N-side. This creates a depletion region (depleted of free carriers) with a built-in electric field that opposes further diffusion — establishing equilibrium.

Forward Bias (P connected to +ve, N to −ve terminal): Applied voltage opposes built-in field → depletion layer NARROWS Majority carriers flow across junction → large current flows Minimum forward voltage to start conduction (knee voltage): Silicon: ~0.7V Germanium: ~0.3V Forward current increases exponentially with voltage. Reverse Bias (P connected to −ve, N to +ve): Applied voltage aids built-in field → depletion layer WIDENS Only minority carriers cross → very small leakage current (~μA) Beyond breakdown voltage → sudden large reverse current (Zener diode exploits this controlled breakdown) V-I Characteristics: Forward: exponential rise after knee voltage Reverse: flat near-zero (leakage), then sharp breakdown

📌 Rectifiers, LED & Solar Cell

Half-Wave Rectifier: Only one diode; conducts only during positive half-cycle. Output: pulsating DC (ripple factor = 1.21, efficiency = 40.6%) Full-Wave Rectifier (Center-tap or Bridge): Conducts both half cycles. Ripple factor = 0.48, efficiency = 81.2% Bridge rectifier uses 4 diodes (no center-tap needed) Light Emitting Diode (LED): Forward-biased P-N junction made of compound semiconductors (GaAs, GaP) Electrons recombine with holes at junction → energy released as PHOTONS Color depends on bandgap: Red (GaAsP), Green (GaP), Blue (GaN) Very efficient, long life, compact, low voltage Used in displays, indicators, traffic lights, lighting Solar Cell (Photovoltaic Effect): Reverse of LED: light energy → electrical energy Photons with energy > bandgap are absorbed and generate electron-hole pairs Built-in field at junction separates them → EMF developed Multiple cells in series/parallel form a solar panel Efficiency: ~15–25% for silicon solar cells
